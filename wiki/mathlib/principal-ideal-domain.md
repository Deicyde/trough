A **principal ideal domain**, or PID, is an integral domain in which every
[ideal](trough:Ideal) is generated by a single element. The [integers](trough:Int) and the
[polynomials](trough:Polynomial) in one variable over a [field](trough:Field) are the standard
examples. In a PID the arithmetic of the integers carries over almost unchanged:
greatest common divisors exist and satisfy Bézout's identity, irreducible
elements are prime, and every nonzero nonunit factors into irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units.
Finitely generated [modules](trough:Module) over a PID also have a complete and explicit
classification, as direct sums of cyclic modules.

## Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative [ring](trough:Ring). An ideal $I$ of $R$ is
principal if $I = (a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ for some element $a$ of $R$,
that is, if $I$ is the ideal [generated](trough:Submodule.span) by one element. The ring $R$ is a
principal ideal ring if every ideal of $R$ is principal, and a
principal ideal domain if moreover it is an integral domain: $1 \neq 0$ and
$ab = 0$ forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.

Principal ideals translate divisibility into inclusion. For elements $x$ and $y$
of $R$,
$(x) \subseteq (y)$ holds exactly when $y$ divides $x$,
and in an integral domain
$(x) = (y)$ holds exactly when $x$ and $y$ are associates,
that is, when $x = uy$ for a unit $u$. So the ideals of a PID correspond to its
elements taken up to multiplication by units.

## Examples

- **Fields.** A field has only the ideals $0 = (0)$ and $(1)$, so
  every field is a principal ideal ring.
- **Euclidean domains.** Every [Euclidean domain](trough:EuclideanDomain) is a
  principal ideal ring, hence a PID:
  in a nonzero ideal $I$ choose a nonzero element $m$ of least size under the
  Euclidean function; dividing any
  $x \in I$ by $m$ leaves a remainder that lies in $I$ and is smaller than $m$,
  hence zero, so $I = (m)$. This covers [the integers](trough:Int.euclideanDomain),
  [the polynomial ring $K[X]$ over a field $K$](trough:Polynomial.instEuclideanDomain),
  and the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. The converse fails: the ring
  $\mathbb{Z}[(1 + \sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a PID that admits no Euclidean function.
- **Non-examples.** In $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the ideal $(2, X)$ is not principal: a
  generator would be a common divisor of $2$ and $X$, and the only such divisors
  are $\pm 1$, yet every
  element of $(2, X)$ has even constant term. Similarly $(X, Y)$ is not principal
  in $K[X, Y]$, and $(2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ is not principal in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.

## Properties

- **Noetherian.** Each ideal is generated by one element, hence finitely
  generated, so every principal ideal ring is Noetherian
  (see [Noetherian ring](trough:IsNoetherianRing)).
- **Greatest common divisors.** In a PID, the ideal $(a, b)$ is principal, and its
  generators are exactly the [greatest common divisors](trough:GCDMonoid.gcd) of $a$ and $b$:
  $(a, b) = (\gcd(a, b))$. Consequently
  $\gcd(a, b) = ax + by$ for some $x, y \in R$,
  which is Bézout's identity.
- **Irreducible elements and maximal ideals.** In a principal ideal ring,
  if $p$ is irreducible then $(p)$ is a maximal ideal.
  In a PID, for $p \neq 0$,
  $p$ is irreducible if and only if $(p)$ is maximal
  (see [irreducible element](trough:Irreducible) and [maximal ideal](trough:Ideal.IsMaximal)).
- **Prime ideals.** In a principal ideal ring,
  a nonzero ideal is prime exactly when it is $(p)$ for a prime element $p$,
  where a nonzero nonunit $p$ is prime if $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.
  In a PID every nonzero prime ideal is maximal
  (see [prime ideal](trough:Ideal.IsPrime)). The Krull dimension, the supremum of
  the lengths of chains of prime ideals, is therefore at most $1$; when $R$ is not a
  field, a nonzero nonunit lies in a maximal ideal, which gives the chain
  $0 \subsetneq \mathfrak{m}$ of prime ideals, so
  a PID that is not a field has Krull dimension exactly $1$.
- **Unique factorisation.**
  Every PID is a unique factorisation domain
  (see [unique factorisation domain](trough:UniqueFactorizationMonoid)). Factorisations into
  irreducibles exist because an ascending chain of principal ideals must
  stabilise, and they are unique because an irreducible $p$ generates a maximal,
  hence prime, ideal, which makes $p$ a prime element. The converse fails:
  $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a unique factorisation domain but not a PID. For Dedekind
  domains (Noetherian, integrally closed domains in which every nonzero prime
  ideal is maximal) the two notions agree:
  a Dedekind domain with unique factorisation is a principal ideal domain.

## Modules over a principal ideal domain

Linear algebra over a field extends to PIDs with one new feature, torsion: an
element $m$ of an $R$-module is torsion if $rm = 0$ for some nonzero $r \in R$, and a
module is torsion-free if $0$ is its only torsion element. Over a
PID, every finitely generated torsion-free module is free,
and every submodule of a free module of finite rank is free of finite rank.
The [structure theorem](trough:Module.equiv_free_prod_directSum) states that
every finitely generated module $M$ over a PID $R$ is isomorphic to a module

$$M \cong R^n \oplus R/(p_1^{e_1}) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(p_k^{e_k})$$

for some $n \ge 0$, irreducible elements $p_1, \dots, p_k$ and natural numbers
$e_1, \dots, e_k$. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$ this classifies finitely generated
abelian groups; for $R = K[X]$, applied to a vector space with a linear
operator, it yields the rational canonical form and, over an algebraically closed field,
the Jordan canonical form.

## Related concepts

The classes of rings discussed here are nested: every
Euclidean domain is a PID, every PID is a unique
factorisation domain, and every unique factorisation domain is integrally
closed, with each inclusion strict. A Bézout domain asks only that finitely
generated ideals be principal, and
a Noetherian Bézout ring is a principal ideal ring. A local PID
that is not a field is a discrete valuation ring, and the Dedekind domains are
exactly the Noetherian integral domains whose [localisations](trough:Localization) at nonzero prime ideals are
discrete valuation rings; in other words, a Dedekind domain is a Noetherian domain
whose localisation at every prime ideal is a principal ideal domain.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib has no separate class of principal ideal domains. Principality is
defined for submodules in general: a submodule is principal when it is spanned by
a single element, and an ideal is a submodule of the ring over itself. A
principal ideal ring is a semiring, not necessarily commutative, in which every
left ideal is principal, and a principal ideal domain is expressed by assuming
both that a ring is an integral domain and that it is a principal ideal ring.
Results that need less are stated with less: the Noetherian property for any
principal ideal ring, and the maximality of the ideal generated by an irreducible
element for any commutative one. The results on greatest common divisors are proved
for Bézout domains, a larger class than PIDs, relative to any chosen
greatest-common-divisor function. A generator of a principal submodule is picked
by choice rather than computed.
