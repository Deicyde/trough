# Cyclic group

A **cyclic group** is a [group](group.md) in which every element is a power of
one fixed element. Cyclic groups are among the most elementary groups: up to
isomorphism there is exactly one finite cyclic group with $n$ elements for each
$n \ge 1$, namely the integers modulo $n$, and one infinite one, the
[integers](../../numbers/integer.md) under addition. They are the
building blocks of the [finitely generated abelian groups](../part-02/structure-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups.md), and they turn up
throughout number theory, most famously as the multiplicative group of
residues modulo a prime.

## Definition

A group $G$ is [cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/DivInvMonoid.html#IsCyclic) if it contains an element $g$ such that
every element of $G$ has the form $g^k$ for some integer $k$. Such an element
is called a generator. In other words, $G$ is cyclic exactly when
[it equals the subgroup generated by a single element](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#isCyclic_iff_exists_zpowers_eq_top)
(see [subgroup generated by a subset](subgroup-generated-by-a-subset.md)). Written
additively, the condition says that every element is an integer multiple
$kg$ of the generator.

For every element $g$ of any group, the set of powers
$\{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a [subgroup](subgroup.md), and
[that subgroup is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#Subgroup.isCyclic_zpowers). Its order is the
[order](../../../../undergraduate/group-theory/basic-definitions/order-of-an-element.md) of $g$. Consequently a finite group $G$ is cyclic
[if and only if it has an element whose order is $|G|$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#isCyclic_iff_exists_orderOf_eq_natCard).

## Examples

- The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition are cyclic, generated by $1$ (or by
  $-1$). Every infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$.
- The integers modulo $n$, written $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, form
  [a cyclic group under addition](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#ZMod.instIsAddCyclic), generated by the
  class of $1$. A residue class $a$ generates it exactly when $\gcd(a, n) = 1$.
- The complex $n$th roots of unity form a cyclic group of order $n$ under
  multiplication, generated by $e^{2\pi i/n}$.
- [Every group of prime order is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#isCyclic_of_prime_card), by
  [Lagrange's theorem](../part-02/lagranges-theorem.md): any element
  other than the identity generates a subgroup whose order divides the prime
  and exceeds $1$.
- The group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, the Klein
  four-group, is not cyclic: it has order $4$, but every element other than the
  identity has order $2$.

## Classification

A cyclic group is determined up to isomorphism by its order. If $g$ generates
$G$, the map $k \mapsto g^k$ is a surjective [group morphism](group-morphism.md) from $\mathbb{Z}$ onto $G$. Its kernel is a subgroup
of $\mathbb{Z}$, hence of the form $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \ge 0$, and the
[first isomorphism theorem](first-isomorphism-theorem.md) gives

$$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.$$

Here $n$ is the order of $G$ when $G$ is finite, and $n = 0$, giving
$\mathbb{Z}$ itself, when $G$ is infinite; this is
[the isomorphism Mathlib constructs](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#zmodCyclicMulEquiv). In particular
[two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#mulEquivOfCyclicCardEq).

## Properties

- **Commutativity.** Powers of one element commute with each other, so
  [every cyclic group is abelian](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Basic.html#IsCyclic.isMulCommutative). The converse
  fails, as the Klein four-group shows.
- **Subgroups and quotients.** [Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#Subgroup.isCyclic): unless it is trivial, it is generated by
  the smallest positive power of the generator that it contains. Likewise
  [the image of a cyclic group under a surjective morphism is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Basic.html#isCyclic_of_surjective), so every [quotient group](quotient-group.md) of a cyclic group is cyclic.
- **The subgroup lattice.** If $G$ is cyclic of order $n$, then for each divisor
  $d$ of $n$ there is a subgroup of order $d$, generated by $g^{n/d}$, and
  [a finite subgroup is determined by its order](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsCyclic.subgroup_eq_iff_card_eq).
  So the subgroups of $G$ correspond one to one with the divisors of $n$.
- **Counting elements.** In a cyclic group of order $n$, for each divisor $d$ of
  $n$ [the number of elements of order $d$ is $\varphi(d)$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsCyclic.card_orderOf_eq_totient),
  where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](../../divisibility-in-integral-domains/eulers-totient-function-varphi.md). Taking
  $d = n$, the group has $\varphi(n)$ generators; summing over all $d$ gives the
  identity
  $$\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n.$$
- **Exponent.** The exponent of a group is the least positive $m$ with $x^m = e$
  for all $x$. For a finite cyclic group
  [the exponent equals the order](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsCyclic.exponent_eq_card).
- **Automorphisms.** An automorphism of a cyclic group is determined by where it
  sends a generator, and the image must again be a generator; conversely, if $g$
  generates $G$ and $h$ is any generator, the map $g^k \mapsto h^k$ is a
  well-defined automorphism. Hence a cyclic group of order
  $n$ [has exactly $\varphi(n)$ automorphisms](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsCyclic.card_mulAut).
- **Products.** The product $M \times N$ of two finite groups
  [is cyclic if and only if both factors are cyclic and their orders are coprime](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#Group.isCyclic_prod_iff). This is the group form of the
  [Chinese remainder theorem](../../ideals-and-quotients/chinese-remainder-theorem.md):
  $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$
  when $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.
- **Simple groups.** An abelian group is [simple](simple-group.md) exactly
  when [its order is prime](../part-02/classification-of-abelian-simple-groups.md), so the
  abelian simple groups are the cyclic groups of prime order.

## Cyclic groups in number theory

The units of a finite field form a cyclic group; more generally,
[every finite subgroup of the group of units of an integral domain is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/IntegralDomain.html#isCyclic_subgroup_units). For a prime $p$ this says that
[the nonzero residues modulo $p$ form a cyclic group under multiplication](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/ZMod/UnitsCyclic.html#ZMod.isCyclic_units_prime); a generator is called a
primitive root modulo $p$. For composite moduli the situation is more
delicate: the [group of units modulo $n$](../../../../undergraduate/ring-theory/divisibility-in-integral-domains/z-n-z-and-its-invertible-elements.md)
for a positive integer $n$
[is cyclic exactly when $n$ is $1$, $2$, $4$, $p^m$ or $2p^m$ for an odd prime $p$ and $m \ge 1$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/ZMod/UnitsCyclic.html#ZMod.isCyclic_units_iff); Mathlib's list also includes
$n = 0$, whose units are $\pm 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. The units modulo $8$, for
instance, form a Klein four-group.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib treats being cyclic as a property of a type rather than as extra
structure: the definition asks only for the existence of a generator, and it is
stated for any type with integer powers, so it applies to groups and to more
general objects alike. The additive version is a separate class, written out
alongside this one and paired with it by Mathlib's translation between
multiplicative and additive notation, and a group is cyclic precisely when its
additive counterpart is.
Sizes are measured with a cardinality that gives $0$ for an infinite set; with
this convention the integers modulo $0$ are the integers, so the classification
above reads uniformly as "a cyclic group of order $n$ is the integers modulo
$n$" for every $n$, finite groups and $\mathbb{Z}$ included. The criterion for
products is likewise stated for arbitrary groups under the same convention.
