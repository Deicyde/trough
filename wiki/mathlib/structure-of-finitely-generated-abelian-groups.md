The **structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups** says that every
abelian [group](trough:Group) generated by finitely many elements is a direct
product of finitely many [cyclic groups](trough:IsCyclic): some copies of the
integers and some finite cyclic groups of prime-power order. It classifies these
groups completely up to isomorphism. Questions about such a group, such as how many
elements of a given order it has or which groups occur as its subgroups, reduce to
questions about a list of integers, and the theorem is the model case of the
[structure theorem](trough:Module.equiv_free_prod_directSum) for finitely
generated modules over a [principal ideal domain](trough:Submodule.IsPrincipal).

## Statement

An abelian group $G$, written additively, is
[finitely generated](lean:AddGroup.FG) if it contains finitely many elements
$g_1, \dots, g_k$ such that every element of $G$ can be written as
$a_1 g_1 + \dots + a_k g_k$ with integers $a_i$. For such a group there are a
natural number $r$, primes $p_1, \dots, p_m$ (not necessarily distinct) and
positive exponents $e_1, \dots, e_m$ such that

$$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \times \mathbb{Z}/p_1^{e_1}\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/p_m^{e_m}\mathbb{Z}.$$

Mathlib states
[the same decomposition with the exponents allowed to be any natural numbers](lean:AddCommGroup.equiv_free_prod_directSum_zmod),
so that some factors may be trivial groups $\mathbb{Z}/p^0\mathbb{Z}$; dropping those
factors gives the display above with positive exponents.
The number $r$ is the **rank** of $G$, and the prime powers $p_i^{e_i}$ are its
**elementary divisors**. Both are determined by $G$ (the elementary divisors
counted with multiplicity and up to order), so two finitely generated abelian groups are isomorphic exactly when
they have the same rank and the same elementary divisors.

Grouping the elementary divisors differently gives an equivalent **invariant
factor** form: $G \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \times \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/d_s\mathbb{Z}$
with integers $d_1, \dots, d_s > 1$ and each $d_i$ dividing $d_{i+1}$. The two forms
are related by the [Chinese remainder theorem](trough:Ideal.quotientInfRingEquivPiQuotient):
[$\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ when $m$ and $n$ are coprime](lean:ZMod.chineseRemainder).

## Examples

- The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ has elementary divisors $4$ and $3$,
  so it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. It is
  not isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, whose
  elementary divisors are $2$, $2$ and $3$.
- Up to isomorphism there are exactly three abelian groups of order $8$, one for
  each partition of $3$ (each way of writing $3$ as a sum of positive integers,
  ignoring order):
  $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and
  $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$. In general the number of abelian groups, up to isomorphism, of order
  $p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_t^{a_t}$ with distinct primes $p_i$ is the product of the numbers of partitions of the
  exponents $a_1, \dots, a_t$.
- The additive group of the rationals is abelian but not finitely generated, and it
  is not a product of cyclic groups: any two nonzero rationals have a common nonzero
  multiple, so it has no nontrivial decomposition at all, and it is not cyclic.

## Consequences

- **Finite abelian groups.** A finite abelian group has rank $0$, so it is
  [a direct sum of cyclic groups of prime-power order](lean:AddCommGroup.equiv_directSum_zmod_of_finite)
  (for finitely many factors the direct sum and the direct product are the same group),
  with trivial factors allowed, and, discarding those,
  [a direct sum of cyclic groups each of order greater than $1$](lean:AddCommGroup.equiv_directSum_zmod_of_finite').
- **Torsion.** In a finitely generated abelian group the elements of finite
  [order](trough:orderOf) form a subgroup $T$, the **torsion subgroup**, isomorphic to the finite part of the
  decomposition. In particular,
  [a finitely generated abelian group in which every element has finite order is finite](lean:AddCommGroup.finite_of_fg_isAddTorsion).
- **Torsion-free groups.** A finitely generated abelian group with no nonzero
  elements of finite order is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^r$ for some $r$; more generally,
  over a principal ideal domain,
  [a finitely generated module is free exactly when it is torsion-free](lean:Module.free_iff_isTorsionFree)
  (that is, $a m = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $m = 0$).
- **A converse to Lagrange's theorem.** For a finite abelian group, every divisor of
  the order is the order of some subgroup. For arbitrary finite groups this converse
  of [Lagrange's theorem](trough:Subgroup.card_subgroup_dvd_card) fails: the
  alternating group $A_4$ of order $12$ has no subgroup of order $6$. In the abelian
  case, write the divisor $d$ as a product $d_1 \cdots d_m$ with each $d_i$ dividing
  the order $n_i$ of the $i$-th cyclic factor (for instance, take
  $d_1 = \gcd(d, n_1)$ and repeat with $d/d_1$ and the remaining factors); a cyclic group has a subgroup of every order
  dividing its own, and the product of the subgroups of order $d_i$ of these factors
  has order $d$.
- **Cyclicity.** A finite abelian group whose order is not divisible by the square of
  any prime is cyclic, since its elementary divisors are distinct primes and the
  Chinese remainder theorem combines them.

## Proof

Choose generators $g_1, \dots, g_k$ of $G$. The map $\mathbb{Z}^k \to G$ sending
$(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ to $a_1 g_1 + \dots + a_k g_k$ is a surjective homomorphism, so
$G \cong \mathbb{Z}^k / K$ for its kernel $K$. Because $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal
domain, [a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^k$ is again free of finite rank](lean:Submodule.basisOfPid).
More precisely, after renumbering,
[there are a basis $f_1, \dots, f_k$ of $\mathbb{Z}^k$ and integers $d_1, \dots, d_s$ such that $d_1 f_1, \dots, d_s f_s$ is a basis of $K$](lean:Submodule.smithNormalForm);
the $d_i$ are nonzero, since the vectors $d_i f_i$ form a basis.
In matrix language this is the Smith normal form of an integer matrix whose columns
generate $K$, where one can also arrange that the $d_i$ are positive and each divides
the next; Mathlib's version does not record that divisibility, and the proof does not
need it. Replacing $d_i$ by $|d_i|$, in these coordinates

$$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/d_s\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^{k-s}.$$

Factoring each $d_i$ into prime powers, using the
[fundamental theorem of arithmetic](trough:Nat.primeFactorsList_unique), and applying
the Chinese remainder theorem splits each finite factor into cyclic groups of
prime-power order. Uniqueness follows by counting: the rank is the rank of the free abelian group
$G/T$, where $T$ is the torsion subgroup, and for each prime $p$ and each $t \ge 1$ the
number of elementary divisors equal to $p^j$ with $j \ge t$ is the dimension over
$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ of the subgroup of elements of
$p^{t-1} G = \{p^{t-1} g : g \in G\}$ that are killed by $p$.

## Generalizations

An abelian group is the same thing as a module over the integers, and
[being finitely generated as a group is the same as being finitely generated as a module over the integers](lean:Module.Finite.iff_addGroup_fg).
The theorem is therefore the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of the statement that
[every finitely generated module over a principal ideal domain $R$ is a free module of finite rank times a direct sum of quotients $R/(p^e)$ with $p$ irreducible](lean:Module.equiv_free_prod_directSum).
The case of the polynomial ring over a field yields the rational canonical form of a
matrix and, when the characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors, its Jordan
canonical form.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib proves the theorem by applying its structure theorem for modules over a
principal ideal domain to the integers. Rather than through Smith
normal form, the module theorem is proved by splitting off the torsion submodule,
whose quotient is torsion-free and hence free, and decomposing the torsion part
first into its primary components and then into cyclic pieces. The free part
$\mathbb{Z}^r$ appears as the group of integer-valued functions on a set of $r$
elements, and the finite part as a direct sum indexed by a finite type. The exponents
are only required to be natural numbers, so the statement allows trivial factors
$\mathbb{Z}/p^0\mathbb{Z}$; a separate version for finite groups removes them, at the cost of stating only that
each cyclic factor has order greater than $1$. Only
the existence of the decomposition is stated, not the uniqueness of the rank and
elementary divisors. A
[multiplicative version](lean:CommGroup.equiv_free_prod_prod_multiplicative_zmod)
states the same result for abelian groups written multiplicatively, with the
direct sum replaced by a product.
