The **third isomorphism theorem** says that taking a quotient of a quotient
group gives nothing new: if $N \le M$ are [normal subgroups](trough:Subgroup.Normal)
of a [group](trough:Group) $G$, then $M/N$ is a normal subgroup of $G/N$, and
dividing $G/N$ by it gives $G/M$. In symbols, $(G/N)/(M/N) \cong G/M$, so the
quotient by $N$ can be removed from both sides. Together with the correspondence
between subgroups of $G/N$ and subgroups of $G$ containing $N$, it shows that
every quotient of $G/N$ is already a quotient of $G$. This lets an argument by
induction on the order of a finite group, or the construction of a composition
series, replace a quotient of a [quotient group](trough:QuotientGroup.Quotient.group)
by a single quotient of the original group.

## Statement

Let $G$ be a group and let $N$ and $M$ be normal subgroups of $G$ with
$N \le M$. Write $M/N$ for the image of $M$ under the quotient map
$G \to G/N$, that is, the set of cosets $mN$ with $m \in M$. Then:

1. $M/N$ is a normal subgroup of $G/N$.
2. The rule $(gN)(M/N) \mapsto gM$
   is a well-defined map and an
   isomorphism of groups
   $$(G/N)/(M/N) \cong G/M.$$

## Examples

- **Integers.** Let $m$ and $n$ be positive integers with $m \mid n$. In the
  additive group $\mathbb{Z}$, every subgroup is normal and
  $n\mathbb{Z} \le m\mathbb{Z}$. The
  additive form of the theorem
  gives
  $$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})/(m\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}.$$
  For $n = 12$ and $m = 4$: the subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ generated by
  the class of $4$ has three elements, and the quotient by it is cyclic of order
  $4$.
- **Symmetric group.** In $S_4$, the Klein four-group $V$ (the identity and the
  three double transpositions) is normal and lies in the alternating group $A_4$,
  which is also normal. Here $S_4/V \cong S_3$ and $A_4/V$ is its subgroup of
  order $3$, so the theorem gives $(S_4/V)/(A_4/V) \cong S_4/A_4$, a group of
  order $2$.
- **Extreme cases.** For $M = G$ both sides are trivial. For $N$ trivial,
  $G/N$ is canonically isomorphic to $G$, with $M/N$ corresponding to $M$, and
  under this identification the statement reduces to $G/M \cong G/M$.

## Proof

Since $N \le M$, the rule $gN \mapsto gM$ is well defined: if $gN = g'N$ then
$g^{-1}g' \in N \subseteq M$, so $gM = g'M$. It is a homomorphism
$\varphi \colon G/N \to G/M$, and it is surjective. Its kernel consists of the
cosets $gN$ with $gM = M$, that is, with $g \in M$; so the kernel is exactly
$M/N$, which is therefore normal in $G/N$. The [first isomorphism
theorem](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange), in the form
a surjective homomorphism induces an isomorphism from the quotient by its kernel
onto its target, turns
$\varphi$ into the isomorphism $(G/N)/(M/N) \cong G/M$.

## Consequences

- **Correspondence theorem.** Taking preimages under the quotient map gives an
  order-preserving bijection between
  the subgroups of $G/N$ and the subgroups of $G$ that contain $N$;
  under it, normal subgroups correspond to normal subgroups, because
  the preimage of a normal subgroup under a homomorphism is normal
  and the image of a normal subgroup under a surjective homomorphism is
  normal. Combined with the
  third isomorphism theorem, this says that the quotients of $G/N$ are, up to
  isomorphism, the quotients $G/M$ with $N \le M$.
- **Simple quotients.** By the correspondence, $G/M$ is a
  [simple group](trough:IsSimpleGroup) exactly when $M$ is a maximal proper
  normal subgroup of $G$. This is how composition series are built and compared,
  as in the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](trough:CompositionSeries.jordan_holder).
- **Indices multiply.** For finite quotients, counting elements in the
  isomorphism gives $|G/N| = |G/M|\,|M/N|$. The underlying identity holds for any
  subgroups $N \le M$, normal or not:
  the index of $N$ in $G$ is the index of $N$ in $M$ times the index of $M$ in
  $G$, that is,
  $$[G : N] = [G : M]\,[M : N].$$

## Generalizations

Analogues hold for any algebraic structure whose quotients are formed by
congruences; for modules and rings, congruences correspond to submodules and
ideals. Mathlib proves several instances.

- **Modules.** For submodules $S \le T$ of a [module](trough:Module) $V$ over a
  ring, $(V/S)/(T/S)$ is isomorphic to $V/T$ as modules;
  every submodule can serve as a kernel, so no normality hypothesis appears.
- **Rings.** For [ideals](trough:Ideal) $I \le J$ of a commutative ring $R$,
  $(R/I)/(J/I)$ is isomorphic to $R/J$ as rings.
- **Monoids.** For congruences $c \le d$ on a monoid, or more generally on any
  set with a multiplication and an identity element, the quotient by $d$ is
  isomorphic to the quotient of the quotient by $c$ by the congruence that $d$
  induces on it.
- **Sets.** Without any algebra, if an equivalence relation $r$ is finer than
  $s$, then the quotient by $s$ is in bijection with a quotient of the quotient
  by $r$.

The other isomorphism theorems are the
[first](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange) and the
[second isomorphism theorem](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientInfEquivProdNormalQuotient).

## In Mathlib

Mathlib states the theorem with $M/N$ taken as the image of $M$ under the
quotient homomorphism $G \to G/N$, which keeps it a subgroup of $G/N$; that this
image is normal is recorded once and found automatically wherever it is needed.
Both $N$ and $M$ must be normal in $G$, and the inclusion $N \le M$ is passed as
a hypothesis. The result is a bundled isomorphism of groups. Unlike the second
isomorphism theorem, it is not obtained from the first isomorphism theorem by a
choice of preimages: Mathlib writes down both directions, $(gN)(M/N) \mapsto gM$
and its inverse $gM \mapsto (gN)(M/N)$, and checks that they are mutually
inverse. The additive version, for additive groups such as $\mathbb{Z}$, is
generated automatically from the multiplicative one. The index identity counts an
infinite index as $0$, so it holds for every group, finite or not.
