The **first isomorphism theorem for commutative rings** says that a surjective
[ring morphism](trough:RingHom) $f \colon R \to S$ identifies the
[quotient ring](trough:Ideal.Quotient.ring) of $R$ by the kernel of $f$ with $S$.
It turns the question of which rings are images of $R$ into the question of
which [ideals](trough:Ideal) $R$ has, and it is the usual way to recognize a
quotient ring: to show that $R/I$ is isomorphic to a known ring $S$, one finds a
surjective morphism from $R$ onto $S$ whose kernel is $I$. It is the ring-theoretic
counterpart of the [first isomorphism theorem for groups](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange).

## Statement

Let $f \colon R \to S$ be a morphism of commutative [rings](trough:Ring). Its kernel
$\ker f = \{ a \in R : f(a) = 0 \}$ is an ideal of $R$, so the
quotient ring $R/\ker f$ is defined. If $f$ is surjective, then
$R/\ker f$ is isomorphic to $S$ as a ring,
by the isomorphism that
sends the coset $a + \ker f$ to $f(a)$:

$$R/\ker f \;\cong\; S, \qquad a + \ker f \mapsto f(a).$$

Without surjectivity, the image $f(R)$ is a subring of $S$, and
$R/\ker f$ is isomorphic to $f(R)$.

## Proof

Write $K = \ker f$ and define $\bar f(a + K) = f(a)$. Two cosets $a + K$ and
$b + K$ are equal exactly when $a - b \in K$, that is, when $f(a) - f(b) = 0$.
Read in one direction, this says $\bar f$ is well defined; read in the other, it
says $\bar f$ is injective. Since sums and products of cosets are computed on
representatives and $f$ preserves sums, products and the identity, $\bar f$ is a
ring morphism. Well-definedness and the morphism property are both instances of the
universal property of the quotient:
a ring morphism that vanishes on an ideal $I$
induces a ring morphism on $R/I$. The morphism induced
on $R/\ker f$ is injective, and it is surjective
because $f$ is. A bijective ring morphism has a ring morphism as its inverse, so
$\bar f$ is an isomorphism.

## Examples

- **Integers modulo $n$.** Reduction modulo $n$ is a surjective morphism from the
  [integers](trough:Int) onto the ring of residues modulo $n$, and
  its kernel is the ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$. Hence
  $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to the ring of residues modulo $n$.
- **Evaluation of polynomials.** For a commutative ring $R$ and $c \in R$,
  evaluation at $c$ maps the [polynomial ring](trough:Polynomial) $R[x]$ onto $R$
  (constants are sent to themselves), and
  its kernel is the ideal generated by $x - c$.
  So $R[x]/(x - c)$ is isomorphic to $R$.
- **The complex numbers.** The morphism $\mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$ sending a
  real polynomial $p$ to $p(i)$ is surjective, since $a + bx$ maps to $a + bi$, and
  its kernel is the ideal generated by $x^2 + 1$, the minimal polynomial of $i$
  over the reals. Hence $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.
- **Products.** The projection $R \times S \to R$ is surjective with kernel
  $\{0\} \times S$, so $(R \times S)/(\{0\} \times S) \cong R$.

## Consequences

- **Ideals are kernels.** Conversely, every ideal $I$ is a kernel:
  the kernel of the quotient map $R \to R/I$ is $I$. Up to
  isomorphism, the surjective images of $R$ are therefore exactly its quotients $R/I$.
- **Prime and maximal kernels.** If $S$ is an integral domain, then $R/\ker f$ is
  one, and indeed for any morphism into a nonzero ring without zero divisors
  the kernel is a prime ideal. If $f$ is surjective and
  $S$ is a field, or more generally a division ring,
  the kernel is a maximal ideal
  (see [prime ideal](trough:Ideal.IsPrime) and [maximal ideal](trough:Ideal.IsMaximal)).
- **Ideals of the image.** For surjective $f$,
  the ideals of $S$ correspond, by taking preimages, to the ideals of $R$ containing $\ker f$,
  and the correspondence preserves inclusion.
- **Third isomorphism theorem.** For ideals $I \subseteq J$ of a commutative ring
  $R$, applying the theorem to the surjection $R/I \to R/J$, whose kernel is the
  image $J/I$ of $J$, gives
  $(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$.
- **[Chinese remainder theorem](trough:Ideal.quotientInfRingEquivPiQuotient).**
  For pairwise coprime ideals $I_1, \dots, I_k$ (that is, $I_i + I_j = R$ whenever
  $i \neq j$), the morphism
  $R \to R/I_1 \times \dots \times R/I_k$ is surjective with kernel
  $I_1 \cap \dots \cap I_k$, which yields the isomorphism of the theorem.

## Generalizations

The same argument gives analogous theorems for groups, modules and algebras, whose
quotients are likewise taken by kernels.
For a surjective group morphism $\varphi \colon G \to H$,
$G/\ker\varphi \cong H$. For a
surjective linear map $f \colon M \to N$ of [modules](trough:Module), the quotient
of $M$ by the [kernel](trough:LinearMap.ker)
is linearly isomorphic to $N$. For a
surjective morphism of [algebras](trough:Algebra) over a commutative ring, the
quotient by the kernel
is isomorphic to the codomain as an algebra.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib states the theorem in more generality than for commutative rings: the
domain may be any ring, commutative or not, and the codomain need only be a
semiring, a structure in which additive inverses need not exist. When the source
ring is noncommutative, the kernel is a two-sided ideal, which is what makes the quotient a ring. The theorem
is a construction rather than an existence statement: it builds a specific ring
isomorphism from the quotient by the kernel to the codomain. Its inverse is
obtained by choosing a preimage of each element, so the definition is
noncomputable; a computable variant
exists for a morphism equipped with a right inverse,
and Mathlib uses it for the integers modulo $n$ and for evaluation of polynomials.
The version onto the image is derived from the surjective one by restricting the
codomain of the morphism to its image, and a further version allows a
semiring as codomain with the image as a subsemiring.
