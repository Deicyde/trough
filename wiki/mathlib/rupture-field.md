A **rupture field** of an [irreducible](trough:Irreducible) polynomial $f$ over a [field](trough:Field) $K$ is an extension of $K$ generated by a single root of $f$. The standard model is the [quotient ring](trough:Ideal.Quotient.ring) $K[X]/(f)$, in which the class of $X$ is a root of $f$ by construction. Applied to an irreducible factor, this quotient construction is how one proves that every nonconstant polynomial over a field has a root in some extension, and iterating it produces [splitting fields](trough:Polynomial.SplittingField). The same quotient makes sense for any polynomial over any commutative ring, and is then called adjoining a root.

## Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative [ring](trough:Ring) and $f$ a [polynomial](trough:Polynomial) in $R[X]$. The ring obtained by adjoining a root of $f$ to $R$ is
the quotient of $R[X]$ by the principal ideal generated by $f$:

$$R[X]/(f).$$

It comes with a ring homomorphism $R \to R[X]/(f)$ sending a constant to its class, and with a distinguished element $\alpha$,
the class of $X$. Every element is the class of some polynomial, and
two polynomials have the same class exactly when $f$ divides their difference. Since $f$ itself has class zero,
$\alpha$ is a root of $f$, where the coefficients of $f$ are read in the new ring.

When $R = K$ is a field, $K[X]/(f)$ is
a field if and only if $f$ is irreducible: in a [principal ideal domain](trough:Submodule.IsPrincipal) that is not a field, such as $K[X]$, the ideal $(f)$ is [maximal](trough:Ideal.IsMaximal) exactly when $f$ is irreducible. For irreducible $f$ the field $K[X]/(f)$ is the rupture field of $f$, and $K$ embeds in it, since a ring homomorphism out of a field is injective.

## Examples

- Over the real numbers, $X^2 + 1$ is irreducible, and $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ is isomorphic to the [complex numbers](trough:Complex), with $\alpha$ corresponding to $i$.
- Over the field with two elements, $X^2 + X + 1$ has degree $2$ and no root, so it is irreducible, and its rupture field is a field with four elements.
- Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the polynomial $X^3 - 2$ is irreducible by [Eisenstein's criterion](trough:Polynomial.irreducible_of_eisenstein_criterion). Its rupture field is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$. The other two complex roots of $X^3 - 2$ are not real, so $X^3 - 2$ does not split in this field: a rupture field need not be a splitting field.
- If $f = X^2$ over a field $K$, the quotient $K[X]/(X^2)$ is the ring of dual numbers. Here $\alpha \neq 0$ but $\alpha^2 = 0$, so the quotient is not a field, matching the reducibility of $X^2$.
- For an element $r$ of a commutative ring $R$, adjoining a root of $rX - 1$
  makes $r$ invertible, with inverse $\alpha$.

## Properties

Let $K$ be a field and $f$ a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over $K$, and write $\alpha$ for the class of $X$ in $L = K[X]/(f)$.

- **Basis and degree.** The powers $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{d-1}$ form a basis of $L$ over $K$, so the dimension of $L$ over $K$ is the degree of $f$. Neither statement needs $f$ to be irreducible.
- **Minimal polynomial.** The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ is $f$ divided by its leading coefficient (see [minimal polynomial](trough:minpoly)).
- **Universal property.** For any commutative ring $R$, polynomial $f$ over $R$, ring homomorphism $i \colon R \to S$ to a commutative ring $S$ and element $s$ of $S$ that is a root of $f$ with coefficients mapped by $i$, there is a ring homomorphism from $R[X]/(f)$ to $S$ that extends $i$ and sends $\alpha$ to $s$. It is unique, because a ring homomorphism out of $R[X]/(f)$ is determined by its values on $R$ and on $\alpha$.
- **Embeddings and roots.** If $E$ is an integral domain that is a $K$-[algebra](trough:Algebra), then
  the $K$-algebra homomorphisms $L \to E$ correspond one to one with the roots of $f$ in $E$, each homomorphism corresponding to the image of $\alpha$. Hence there are at most $d$ of them. For $f = X^3 - 2$ and $E = \mathbb{C}$ there are exactly three, one for each complex [root](trough:Polynomial.roots).
- **Uniqueness.** If $E$ is a field extension of $K$ and $\beta \in E$ is algebraic over $K$ (equivalently, integral: a root of a monic polynomial over $K$) with minimal polynomial $p$, then
  $K[X]/(p)$ is isomorphic as a $K$-algebra to the subfield $K(\beta)$ generated by $\beta$, by
  an isomorphism sending $\alpha$ to $\beta$. If $\beta$ is a root of an irreducible $f$, so that $K(\beta)$ is a rupture field of $f$, then $f$ divided by its leading coefficient is monic, irreducible and has $\beta$ as a root, so it is the minimal polynomial $p$ of $\beta$. Since $(f) = (p)$, the field $K(\beta)$ is isomorphic over $K$ to $K[X]/(f)$; hence any two rupture fields of $f$ are isomorphic over $K$. The isomorphism depends on the choice of root and is not unique in general.

Over a general commutative ring the quotient behaves less uniformly. If $f$ is monic of degree $d$, then
$R[X]/(f)$ is a free $R$-module with basis $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{d-1}$. If $R$ is an integral domain and $f$ is not a nonzero constant,
the map from $R$ to $R[X]/(f)$ is injective, and if $f$ is a prime element of $R[X]$,
the quotient is an integral domain.

## Related concepts

A splitting field of $f$ is obtained by repeatedly adjoining a root of an irreducible factor of what remains of $f$; the rupture field is the first step of that process. By the [primitive element theorem](trough:Field.exists_primitive_element), every finite separable extension is generated by one element, so it is a rupture field of that element's minimal polynomial. Adjoining a root of $rX - 1$ is one way to build the [localization](trough:Localization) of a ring at the powers of $r$.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib does not define rupture fields separately. Its construction works for an arbitrary polynomial over an arbitrary commutative ring, as the quotient by the [ideal](trough:Ideal) generated by $f$, with no irreducibility assumed. When the base is a field and irreducibility of $f$ is supplied as a standing fact, the quotient carries a field structure. Results about bases, degree and minimal polynomials do not use this standing fact and need only that $f$ is nonzero; the dimension formula holds even for $f = 0$, because the quotient is then infinite-dimensional, whose dimension Mathlib records as $0$, and the natural-number degree of the zero polynomial is also $0$. Separately, Mathlib has a structure recording that a given ring is presented as $R[X]/(f)$, through a surjective algebra map from $R[X]$ whose kernel is the ideal generated by $f$; this lets results about the construction apply to rings that are only isomorphic to it.
