The **perfect closure** of a commutative ring $K$ of prime [characteristic](trough:CharP) $p$ is the
ring obtained from $K$ by formally adjoining a $p$-th root, a $p^2$-th root, and so on, of every
element; along the way every nilpotent element of $K$ is sent to $0$.
It is the most economical way to make the [Frobenius morphism](trough:frobenius) $x \mapsto x^p$
bijective: it is a perfect ring that receives a map from $K$, and every ring homomorphism from $K$
into a perfect ring of characteristic $p$ extends to it in exactly one way. For a
[field](trough:Field) it gives a perfect field containing $K$, over which every irreducible
polynomial is [separable](trough:Polynomial.Separable).

## Definition

Fix a [prime](trough:Prime) $p$. A commutative ring of characteristic $p$ is called perfect when
its $p$-th power map is bijective, so that every element has exactly one $p$-th
root.

Let $K$ be a commutative ring of characteristic $p$. A pair $(n, x)$ with $n \ge 0$ an integer and
$x \in K$ is meant to stand for the formal root $x^{1/p^n}$. Since $x^{1/p^n}$ and
$(x^p)^{1/p^{n+1}}$ ought to be the same element, the
perfect closure $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is the set of pairs modulo the equivalence relation generated by

$$(n, x) \sim (n+1, x^p).$$

Every element of $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is the class of some pair.
The ring operations bring two roots to a common level:
the product of the classes of $(m, x)$ and $(n, y)$ is the class of $(m+n,\, x^{p^n} y^{p^m})$,
and their sum is the class of $(m+n,\, x^{p^n} + y^{p^m})$; the sum
formula is correct because the Frobenius morphism is additive. With these operations
$K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is a commutative ring
of characteristic $p$, and
sending $x$ to the class of $(0, x)$ is a ring homomorphism $K \to K^{\mathrm{perf}}$.

Two pairs $(m, x)$ and $(n, y)$ define the same element exactly when $x^{p^{n+k}} = y^{p^{m+k}}$ for some $k \ge 0$.
If $K$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements, the Frobenius morphism of $K$ is injective and
one may take $k = 0$.

## Properties

- **Perfectness.** The ring $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is perfect:
  the inverse of Frobenius sends the class of $(n, x)$ to the class of $(n+1, x)$. Iterating,
  the $p^n$-th power of the class of $(n, x)$ is the image of $x$,
  so the class of $(n, x)$ is the unique $p^n$-th root of the image of $x$.
- **Universal property.** If $L$ is a perfect commutative ring of characteristic $p$, then
  restriction along $K \to K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is a bijection from ring homomorphisms $K^{\mathrm{perf}} \to L$ to ring homomorphisms $K \to L$.
  This is the precise sense in which the perfect closure is the smallest perfect ring over $K$.
- **No nilpotents.** The ring $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements,
  even when $K$ has some. In a perfect ring the map $y \mapsto y^{p^n}$ is injective, and a
  nilpotent $y$ satisfies $y^{p^n} = 0$ for large $n$, so $y = 0$. In particular every nilpotent
  element of $K$ maps to $0$.
- **Fields.** If $K$ is a field, then $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is a field
  and a perfect field, and the map from $K$ is injective,
  since by the equality criterion its kernel consists of elements with some $p^n$-th power equal
  to $0$, and in a field only $0$ is such an element.

## Examples

- If $K$ is already perfect, for instance a [finite field](trough:Mathlib.FieldTheory.Finite.Basic),
  the map $K \to K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ is an isomorphism: the class of $(n, x)$ equals the image of
  the $p^n$-th root of $x$ in $K$.
- For the field of [rational functions](trough:RatFunc) $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, which is not perfect
  because $t$ has no $p$-th root, the perfect closure is the union, inside an algebraic closure of
  $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, of the increasing chain of fields $\mathbb{F}_p(t^{1/p^n})$ for $n \ge 0$.
- For the [polynomial ring](trough:Polynomial) $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$, the perfect closure is the ring of
  polynomial expressions in $t$ whose exponents are nonnegative rational numbers with a power of $p$
  as denominator.
- In $\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^p)$ the class of $x$ is nilpotent, so it maps to $0$, and the perfect
  closure is $\mathbb{F}_p$.

## Perfect closures in general

The universal property determines $K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ up to unique isomorphism, and there is an
intrinsic test for it. Call a ring homomorphism $i \colon K \to L$ into a perfect ring $L$ of
characteristic $p$ a perfect closure of $K$ when every element of $L$ has some $p^n$-th power in the
image of $i$, and every element of the kernel of $i$ has some $p^n$-th power equal to $0$.
The homomorphism $K \to K^{\mathrm{perf}}$ satisfies both conditions.
For any perfect closure, the kernel of $i$ is exactly the $p$-nilradical of $K$,
the ideal of elements with some $p^n$-th power equal to $0$, which for prime $p$
is the nilradical of $K$. If $i \colon K \to L$ and $j \colon K \to M$
are two perfect closures, then $L$ and $M$ are isomorphic as rings,
by an isomorphism that carries $i$ to $j$.

For fields there is also a relative version. If $E$ is an extension field of $F$, the
relative perfect closure of $F$ in $E$ consists of the elements of $E$ having some $p^n$-th power in $F$,
where $F$ has characteristic $p$. It is an intermediate field, and
an intermediate field lies inside it exactly when it is purely inseparable over $F$,
so it is the largest purely inseparable subextension. When $E$ is perfect, for example an
[algebraic closure](trough:AlgebraicClosure) of $F$,
the relative perfect closure is a perfect field, and it is
then a perfect closure of $F$ in the sense above.

## Related concepts

The [perfection](trough:Perfection) of a ring of characteristic $p$ is the dual construction: instead
of adjoining $p$-th roots, its elements are the compatible sequences $(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ of
elements of the ring with $x_{n+1}^p = x_n$ for all $n$, and it is the universal perfect ring mapping to the given ring rather than from it.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib constructs the perfect closure for any commutative ring whose characteristic is a prime
$p$, with $p$ given explicitly. It is the quotient of the set of pairs by the relation that joins
each $(n, x)$ to $(n+1, x^p)$, and equality is then governed by the equivalence relation this
generates. The structures of commutative ring, field and perfect ring are supplied separately,
the field structure only when the input is a field. The general notion of a perfect closure is
phrased with the exponential characteristic, which is $1$ in characteristic zero; with that
convention a perfect closure of a ring of characteristic zero is the same as an isomorphism. A
perfect ring is defined by bijectivity of the $p$-th power map, and the relative perfect closure is
defined for every field extension, using the exponential characteristic of the base field, so that
in characteristic zero it is just the base field.
