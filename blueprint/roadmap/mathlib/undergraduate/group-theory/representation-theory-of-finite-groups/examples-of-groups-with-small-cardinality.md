# Examples of groups with small cardinality

The **groups of small cardinality** are the finite [groups](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-01/group.md) with few elements,
listed up to isomorphism. For each order $n \le 8$ the list is short and can be
derived by hand from [Lagrange's theorem](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/lagranges-theorem.md),
[Cauchy's theorem](../../../thousand-plus/algebra/20-group-theory-and-generalizations/part-01/cauchys-theorem-group-theory.md) and elementary arguments about the
[order of an element](../basic-definitions/order-of-an-element.md). These groups are the standard first examples of the
subject: they show where commutativity first fails, how two groups of the same order can
differ, and they are the test cases on which the representation theory of finite groups is
usually first worked out.

## Groups of prime order

If $|G| = p$ is prime, then [$G$ is cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#isCyclic_of_prime_card), since any
element other than the identity $e$ generates a subgroup whose order divides $p$ and exceeds
$1$. As [two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#mulEquivOfCyclicCardEq),
[any two groups of the same prime order are isomorphic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#mulEquivOfPrimeCardEq): up to
isomorphism the only group of order $p$ is the [cyclic group](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-01/cyclic-group.md)
$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. This settles the orders $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$; the order $1$
belongs to the trivial group alone.

## Order four

For a prime $p$, [every group of order $p^2$ is abelian](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/PGroup.html#IsPGroup.isMulCommutative_of_card_eq_prime_sq),
and such a group [fails to be cyclic exactly when its exponent is $p$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#not_isCyclic_iff_exponent_eq_prime),
that is, when $g^p = e$ for every element $g$. For $p = 2$ a group of order four is therefore either cyclic or of exponent two,
and each kind is unique up to isomorphism.

- The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, which has an element of order $4$.
- The **Klein four-group** $V$, [a group of order four and exponent two](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/KleinFour.html#IsKleinFour), so
  that every element is its own inverse. It can be realised as
  $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, or as the symmetries of a
  non-square rectangle, and [it is not cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/KleinFour.html#IsKleinFour.not_isCyclic).
  [Any two Klein four-groups are isomorphic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/KleinFour.html#IsKleinFour.nonempty_mulEquiv).

More generally,
[a group of exponent two is abelian](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Exponent.html#mul_comm_of_exponent_two): from $(ab)^2 = e$ one gets
$ab = b^{-1}a^{-1} = ba$. Together with the prime case, this shows that every group of order
less than $6$ is abelian.

## Order six

The order six is the first at which a non-abelian group appears. The
[symmetric group](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/permutation-group-of-a-type.md) $S_3$ of all permutations of three letters
[has $3! = 6$ elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Data/Finite/Perm.html#Nat.card_perm), and the symmetric group on a finite set
[is abelian exactly when the set has at most two elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Data/Finite/Perm.html#Equiv.Perm.isMulCommutative_iff_card_le_two).
The group $S_3$ is also the dihedral group $D_3$ of symmetries of an equilateral triangle.

Every group of order six is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ or to $S_3$. By
[Cauchy's theorem](../../../thousand-plus/algebra/20-group-theory-and-generalizations/part-01/cauchys-theorem-group-theory.md) such a group $G$ has an element $a$ of
order $3$ and an element $b$ of order $2$. The subgroup generated by $a$ has [index](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-01/index-of-a-subgroup.md) $2$, so it
is a [normal subgroup](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-01/normal-subgroup.md), and $bab^{-1}$ is $a$ or $a^{-1}$. In the first
case $G$ is abelian and $ab$ has order $6$, so $G$ is cyclic; in the second, $G$ satisfies the
relations of $D_3$ and, having six elements, is isomorphic to it.

## Order eight

There are five groups of order eight. Three are abelian: $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$,
$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$. They
are pairwise non-isomorphic, as the largest order of an element is $8$, $4$ and $2$
respectively. This matches two general facts: every
[finite abelian group is isomorphic to a direct product of finitely many cyclic groups](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/FiniteAbelian/Basic.html#CommGroup.equiv_prod_multiplicative_zmod_of_finite)
(the [structure theorem](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/structure-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups.md)), and for finite cyclic groups
$A$ and $B$ the [direct product](../basic-definitions/direct-product-of-groups.md) $A \times B$
[is cyclic exactly when the orders of $A$ and $B$ are coprime](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#Group.isCyclic_prod_iff).

Two are non-abelian.

- The dihedral group $D_4$ of symmetries of a square. For $n \ge 3$ the dihedral group $D_n$ is
  the symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon; for every $n \ge 1$ the group $D_n$, defined by the
  same generators and relations, [has $2n$ elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Dihedral.html#DihedralGroup.nat_card) (some texts
  write $D_{2n}$ for it), and it
  [is abelian exactly when $n$ is $1$ or $2$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Dihedral.html#DihedralGroup.commutative_iff).
- The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$.
  It is the case $n = 2$ of the dicyclic groups, which for $n \ge 1$
  [have $4n$ elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Quaternion.html#QuaternionGroup.card).

The two are told apart by their elements of order $2$: $D_4$ has five (four reflections and
the rotation by a half turn), while $Q_8$ has only $-1$.

## Larger orders

The number of groups of order $n$, up to isomorphism, for $n = 1, 2, \dots, 15$ is
$1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 5, 2, 2, 1, 5, 1, 2, 1$. The two groups of order $9$ are
$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ and $(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^2$; for orders $10$ and $14$ they are the
cyclic group and the dihedral group $D_5$ or $D_7$. The five groups of order $12$ are the two
abelian groups $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,
the [alternating group](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/alternating-group.md) $A_4$, the dihedral group $D_6$ and the dicyclic
group of order $12$. Every group of order $15$ is cyclic.

## Role in representation theory

These groups are the usual first examples of character tables. A finite abelian group of order
$n$ has exactly $n$ irreducible complex representations up to isomorphism, all of dimension one (see
[representations of abelian groups](representations-of-abelian-groups.md)).
The group $S_3$ has three: the trivial and sign representations and a two-dimensional one, and
$1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6$. The groups $D_4$ and $Q_8$ each have four one-dimensional irreducible
representations and one of dimension two, and they have the same character table although they
are not isomorphic, so the character table does not determine a group.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib has no list of the groups of small order: it has no theorem classifying the groups of
order six or eight, and no count of isomorphism classes of groups of a given order. It has the
pieces from which such a list is built: the cyclic groups (the integers modulo $n$, viewed
multiplicatively), the Klein four-group as a property of a group (order four and exponent two,
with every two such groups isomorphic), the dihedral groups, the dicyclic groups (which Mathlib calls the generalised quaternion
groups) and the symmetric and alternating groups, together with the theorems above on groups of prime order and
of order $p^2$. Sizes are mostly natural-number cardinalities, with $0$ for an infinite group;
the count for the dicyclic groups is instead stated only for those that are finite. The
dihedral and dicyclic groups are defined for every natural number index, and those with index
$0$ are infinite; the count of dihedral group elements covers index $0$ through the size
convention, the count for dicyclic groups assumes a nonzero index, and the statement on which
dihedral groups are abelian covers the infinite one too. The quaternion group is the dicyclic
group with index $2$. The statement on cyclic direct products holds for all groups, finite or
not, under the same convention for sizes.
