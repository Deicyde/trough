The **alternating group** $A_n$ is the group of even permutations of $n$ letters,
a [subgroup](trough:Subgroup) of index two in the symmetric group $S_n$ when
$n \ge 2$. The alternating groups $A_n$ with $n \ge 5$ form the most elementary
infinite family of nonabelian [simple groups](trough:IsSimpleGroup): each has no
normal subgroups other than the trivial group and itself. This fact is the group-theoretic reason why the general polynomial equation
of degree five or more cannot be solved by radicals, and the alternating groups form
one of the infinite families in the classification of finite simple
groups.

## Definition

A transposition is a permutation that exchanges two letters and fixes the rest.
[Every permutation of a finite set can be written as a product of
transpositions](trough:Equiv.Perm.truncSwapFactors), and although such a product is not unique, the parity of the number of transpositions is.
The [sign](trough:Equiv.Perm.sign) of a permutation is $+1$ if this number is even
and $-1$ if it is odd, and it defines a [group morphism](trough:MonoidHom) from the
[permutation group](trough:Equiv.Perm) $S_n$ to the two-element group $\{+1, -1\}$.

The alternating group on a finite set $X$ is
[the kernel of the sign morphism](lean:alternatingGroup), so that
[a permutation belongs to it exactly when its sign is $+1$](lean:Equiv.Perm.mem_alternatingGroup).
Equivalently, [a product of transpositions lies in the alternating group if and only
if the number of factors is even](lean:Equiv.Perm.prod_list_swap_mem_alternatingGroup_iff_even_length).
The group $A_n$ is the alternating group on $\{1, \dots, n\}$; a bijection between
two finite sets [induces an isomorphism between their alternating
groups](lean:Equiv.altCongrHom), so only the number of letters matters.

## Examples

- For $n \le 1$ the symmetric group is trivial, and so is $A_n$. For $n = 2$ the only
  nontrivial permutation is a transposition, which is odd, so $A_2$ is again trivial.
- $A_3$ consists of the identity and the two 3-cycles, the permutations that send
  one letter $a$ to $b$, $b$ to $c$ and $c$ back to $a$; it is cyclic of order $3$.
- $A_4$ has order $12$. It contains the identity, eight 3-cycles and the three
  products of two disjoint transpositions; those three products together with the
  identity form a copy of the Klein four-group $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, which is
  [a normal subgroup of $A_4$](lean:alternatingGroup.normal_kleinFour). The group $A_4$
  is a standard example showing that the converse of [Lagrange's
  theorem](trough:Subgroup.card_subgroup_dvd_card) fails: it has no subgroup of order $6$.
- $A_5$ has order $60$ and is the smallest nonabelian simple group. It is isomorphic to
  the group of rotations of a regular icosahedron.

## Properties

- **Order and index.** For $n \ge 2$, $A_n$ has [index](trough:Subgroup.index)
  [two in $S_n$](lean:alternatingGroup.index_eq_two), so
  [its order is $n!/2$](lean:card_alternatingGroup). Being of index two, it is
  [a normal subgroup](lean:alternatingGroup.normal) of $S_n$ (see [normal
  subgroup](trough:Subgroup.Normal)), and it is
  [the only subgroup of index two](lean:Equiv.Perm.eq_alternatingGroup_of_index_eq_two).
- **Generators.** [Every 3-cycle is even](lean:Equiv.Perm.IsThreeCycle.mem_alternatingGroup),
  since it is the product of two transpositions, and moreover [the 3-cycles generate $A_n$](lean:Equiv.Perm.closure_three_cycles_eq_alternating)
  (see [subgroup generated by a subset](trough:Subgroup.closure)): any product of two
  transpositions can be rewritten as a product of 3-cycles.
- **Small cases.** [$A_n$ is abelian if and only if $n \le 3$](lean:alternatingGroup.isMulCommutative_iff_card_le_three),
  and for $n \ge 4$ [its center is trivial](lean:alternatingGroup.center_eq_bot): only the identity
  commutes with every element. For $n \le 4$ the group
  [is solvable](lean:alternatingGroup.isSolvable), meaning that it has a chain of
  subgroups, each normal in the next, whose successive quotients are abelian.
- **Simplicity.** For $n \ge 5$, [$A_n$ is a simple group](lean:alternatingGroup.isSimpleGroup);
  this theorem has [its own article](trough:alternatingGroup.isSimpleGroup). For $A_5$ a
  short argument uses [conjugacy classes](trough:ConjClasses): their sizes are $1$, $15$,
  $20$, $12$ and $12$, a normal subgroup is a union of classes containing the identity,
  and no such union other than $\{e\}$ and the whole group has an order dividing $60$, as Lagrange's theorem requires.
  The case $n = 4$ shows that the bound is sharp, because of the normal Klein four-group.
- **Commutators.** For $n \ge 5$, $A_n$ is [equal to its own commutator
  subgroup](lean:commutator_alternatingGroup_eq_top), the subgroup generated by all
  commutators $ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$, and
  [it is the commutator subgroup of $S_n$](lean:alternatingGroup.commutator_perm_eq).
  The second statement holds for every $n$, since $S_n / A_n$ is abelian and the
  commutators of transpositions produce all 3-cycles. In particular the
  [abelianization](trough:Abelianization) of $S_n$ is $S_n / A_n$, of order two
  when $n \ge 2$.
- **Transitivity.** The action of $A_n$ on the $n$ letters
  [is $(n - 2)$-fold transitive](lean:alternatingGroup.isMultiplyPretransitive): any
  ordered list of $n - 2$ distinct letters can be carried to any other.

## Related concepts

Since $S_n$ is not solvable for $n \ge 5$ (its normal subgroup $A_n$ is simple and
nonabelian), Galois theory shows that a polynomial of degree $n \ge 5$ over a field
of characteristic zero whose Galois group is the full symmetric group $S_n$ cannot be
solved by radicals (see [solvability by radicals implies a solvable Galois
group](trough:isSolvable_gal_minpoly)); applying this to a suitable quintic over the
rationals gives the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](trough:AbelRuffini.exists_not_solvable_by_rad). Through
[Cayley's theorem](trough:Equiv.Perm.subgroupOfMulAction) every finite group
embeds in some symmetric group, and composing with the embedding of $S_n$ into
$A_{n+2}$ shows that every finite group also embeds in an alternating group. The
quotient $S_n / A_n$ is the [quotient group](trough:QuotientGroup.Quotient.group)
that the [first isomorphism theorem](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange)
identifies, for $n \ge 2$, with the image $\{+1, -1\}$ of the sign morphism.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines the alternating group for any finite type with decidable equality,
as a subgroup of the full permutation group of that type, namely the kernel of the
sign morphism; the textbook $A_n$ is the case of a type with exactly $n$ elements.
Statements that depend on $n$ are phrased through the number of elements of the type.
The formulas for the index and the order assume at least two elements: with fewer,
[the index is $1$](lean:alternatingGroup.index_eq_one), and the order formula would
fail, since $1/2$ rounds down to $0$ in the division of natural numbers. Mathlib proves simplicity for $n \ge 5$ through Iwasawa's
criterion, applied to the action on three-element subsets, with the action on
four-element subsets covering the case of six letters. Its statement that $A_n$ is
the commutator subgroup of $S_n$ carries the hypothesis $n \ge 5$, while the
inclusion of the commutator subgroup in $A_n$ is proved for every $n$.
