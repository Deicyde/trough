The **integers** are the whole numbers $\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots$: the
[natural numbers](trough:Nat), their negatives, and zero. Together they form a set
written $\mathbb{Z}$. They extend the natural numbers so that subtraction is always
possible, and they are the basic example of a commutative ring. Divisibility,
primes and congruences are studied in $\mathbb{Z}$, and every ring receives exactly
one structure-preserving map from it.

## Definition

One standard construction builds $\mathbb{Z}$ from the natural numbers by formal
differences. A pair $(a, b)$ of natural numbers stands for $a - b$, and two pairs
$(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ are identified when $a + d = b + c$. Addition and
multiplication are given by

$$(a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c,\ b + d), \qquad (a, b)\,(c, d) = (ac + bd,\ ad + bc),$$

and these rules respect the identification. The natural number $n$ is the class of
$(n, 0)$, and the negative of the class of $(a, b)$ is the class of $(b, a)$.

Every class contains a pair with a zero entry, so each integer is either a natural
number $n$ or a negative number $-(n + 1)$ with $n$ natural, and never both. This
description can itself serve as the definition, and it is the one used for
the integers in Lean.

## Algebraic structure

With the usual operations, $\mathbb{Z}$ is
a commutative ring (see
[ring structure on $\mathbb{Z}$](trough:Int.instCommRing)): addition and
multiplication are associative and commutative with identities $0$ and $1$, every
integer $a$ has an additive inverse $-a$, and multiplication distributes over
addition. Under addition alone the integers form an abelian [group](trough:Group),
and the subgroup generated by $1$ is all of $\mathbb{Z}$,
so this group is [cyclic](trough:IsCyclic).

The integers form an integral domain: $0 \neq 1$, and a
product $ab$ is zero only when $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. Multiplicative inverses are rare:
an integer is invertible in $\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when it is $1$ or $-1$.

For every [ring](trough:Ring) $R$ there is
a ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $R$, sending a
positive integer $n$ to the sum $1 + \dots + 1$ of $n$ copies of the multiplicative
identity $1$ of $R$, $0$ to $0$, and $-n$ to the negative of that sum, and
every ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $R$ equals this one.
In the language of categories, $\mathbb{Z}$ is the initial ring. Inverting the
nonzero integers gives the [rational numbers](trough:Rat):
$\mathbb{Q}$ is the field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}$
(see [field of fractions](trough:IsFractionRing)).

## Order

The integers are totally ordered, and the order is
compatible with the ring operations: $a \le b$ implies $a + c \le b + c$, and a
product of positive integers is positive; together with $0 < 1$, this makes $\mathbb{Z}$
a strictly ordered ring (see
[ordered ring](trough:IsOrderedRing), of which this is the strict form).

Unlike the natural numbers, $\mathbb{Z}$ has no least element, but
every nonempty set of integers that is bounded below has a least element,
and likewise
every nonempty set bounded above has a greatest element.
Induction extends in both directions:
a property that holds for $0$, passes from $n$ to $n + 1$ and from $-n$ to $-n - 1$ for every natural number $n$, holds for every integer.

The set $\mathbb{Z}$ is infinite but
can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers;
one such correspondence lists the integers as $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, \dots$.

## Divisibility and primes

For integers $a$ and $b$ with $b \neq 0$ there is exactly one pair of integers
$q$, $r$ with $a = bq + r$ and $0 \le r < |b|$,
namely the quotient and the remainder of $a$ by $b$.
This division with remainder makes $\mathbb{Z}$
a Euclidean domain (see
[Euclidean domain](trough:EuclideanDomain) and
[$\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean ring](trough:Int.euclideanDomain)). Repeated division
computes greatest common divisors, and
the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$ can be written $ax + by$ for some integers $x$ and $y$,
which is [Bézout's theorem](trough:Nat.gcd_eq_gcd_ab).

Since every ideal of a Euclidean domain is principal,
$\mathbb{Z}$ is a [principal ideal domain](trough:Submodule.IsPrincipal): its ideals
are the sets $n\mathbb{Z}$ of multiples of a natural number $n$. An integer $p$ is
[prime](trough:Prime) if it is neither zero nor a unit and, whenever $p$ divides a
product $ab$, it divides $a$ or $b$.
An integer is prime exactly when its absolute value is a prime natural number,
so the primes of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $\pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 5, \dots$. Because
every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain
(see [unique factorization domain](trough:UniqueFactorizationMonoid)), every
nonzero integer is $\pm 1$ times a product of positive primes (the empty product
for $\pm 1$), unique up to the order of the factors: the
[fundamental theorem of arithmetic](trough:Nat.primeFactorsList_unique).

For a fixed nonzero integer $n$, [congruence modulo $n$](trough:Int.ModEq) relates integers
with the same remainder by $n$;
$a \equiv b \pmod n$ exactly when $n$ divides $b - a$.

## Related concepts

The integers are the first step in the chain
$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$
leading to the [real numbers](trough:Real) and the
[complex numbers](trough:Complex). Completing $\mathbb{Z}$ with respect to
the $p$-adic absolute value for a prime $p$, under which an integer is small when it
is divisible by a high power of $p$, gives the [$p$-adic integers](trough:PadicInt).

## In Mathlib

The type of integers is defined in Lean's core library rather than in Mathlib,
as the second description above: an integer is either a natural number or the
negative of the successor of one. Every integer therefore has exactly one
representation, and no quotient is needed. The compiler stores small integers
directly and larger ones in arbitrary precision. Mathlib supplies the ring, order
and Euclidean structures as instances on this type. A natural number is not
literally an integer; it becomes one through the cast map, and the absolute value
used for primes is a natural number. Division on $\mathbb{Z}$ follows the
convention of the remainder theorem above, so the remainder is never negative:
$-7$ divided by $2$ gives quotient $-4$ and remainder $1$, rather than the
truncated quotient $-3$. Division is total, and
dividing by zero gives $0$. The greatest common divisor of
two integers is likewise a natural number.
