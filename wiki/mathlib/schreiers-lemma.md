**Schreier's lemma** gives an explicit set of generators for a [subgroup](trough:Subgroup) of a
[group](trough:Group), built from a set of generators of the whole group and one chosen
representative from each coset of the subgroup. Its best-known consequence is that a subgroup
of finite [index](trough:Subgroup.index) in a finitely generated group is itself finitely
generated. The generators it produces, often called Schreier generators, are used in the study
of subgroups of free groups and in algorithms of computational group theory such as the
Schreier–Sims algorithm.

## Statement

Let $G$ be a group, $H$ a subgroup of $G$, and $S$ a subset that generates $G$, meaning that the
[subgroup generated by $S$](trough:Subgroup.closure) is all of $G$. A right transversal of $H$
is a subset $R \subseteq G$ that meets every right coset $Hg$ in exactly one element;
equivalently, every element of $G$ can be written in exactly one way as $hr$ with $h \in H$ and
$r \in R$ (in this case $H$ and $R$ are complements). Such a
transversal always exists and can be chosen to contain the identity.
For $g \in G$ write $\overline{g}$ for the element of $R$ lying in the coset $Hg$.

Schreier's lemma says that if $1 \in R$, then

$$H = \left\langle\, rs\,\overline{rs}^{\,-1} \;:\; r \in R,\ s \in S \,\right\rangle,$$

that is, the elements $rs\,\overline{rs}^{\,-1}$ generate $H$.
Each such element lies in $H$, because $g$ and $\overline{g}$ lie in the same right coset of $H$.

## Examples

- **Integers.** Take $G = \mathbb{Z}$ under addition, $S = \{1\}$, $H = n\mathbb{Z}$ for some
  $n \ge 1$, and $R = \{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$. Written additively, the generators are
  $r + 1 - \overline{r+1}$. For $r < n-1$ this is $0$, and for $r = n-1$ it is $n$, so the lemma
  recovers the fact that $n\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by $n$.
- **A subgroup of a free group.** Let $G$ be the [free group](trough:FreeGroup) on $a$ and $b$,
  with $S = \{a, b\}$, and let $H$ consist of the words in which the exponents of $a$ add up to an even number. Then
  $H$ has index $2$ and $R = \{1, a\}$ is a right transversal. The four generators are $1$, $b$,
  $a^2$ and $aba^{-1}$, so $H$ is generated by $b$, $a^2$ and $aba^{-1}$.

The generating set often contains the identity and repeated or redundant elements, so it is
usually far from the smallest possible generating set.

## Consequences

- **Counting generators.** If $S$ is finite and $H$ has finite index $n$, the lemma uses at most
  $n\,|S|$ generators. Hence $H$ can be generated by at most $[G : H]\,|S|$ elements
  (formal statement).
- **Finite generation.** A subgroup of finite index in a finitely generated group is finitely generated.
  The finite index hypothesis matters: the free group on two generators has subgroups that
  are not finitely generated, such as the normal subgroup generated by $b$.
- **Rank.** If $G$ is finitely generated and $d(G)$ is the least number of generators of $G$, then
  $d(H) \le [G : H]\,d(G)$ for every subgroup $H$ of finite index
  (formal statement).
- **Finitely many commutators.** If a group has only finitely many commutators
  $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$, then its commutator subgroup is finite, and
  its order is bounded by a function of the number of commutators.
  The proof passes to a finitely generated subgroup with the same commutators and applies the
  rank bound above to the intersection of its center with its commutator subgroup. The result
  is closely related to Schur's theorem, which says that a group whose center has finite index
  has a finite commutator subgroup.

## Proof

Let $U$ be the set of elements $rs\,\overline{rs}^{\,-1}$ and $K$ the subgroup it generates. Every
element of $U$ lies in $H$, so $K \le H$.

The main step is that every element of $G$ is a product $kr$ with $k \in K$ and $r \in R$.
The set $KR$ contains $1 = 1 \cdot 1$. It is closed under multiplication on the right by each
$s \in S$, since

$$krs = k\,\bigl(rs\,\overline{rs}^{\,-1}\bigr)\,\overline{rs}.$$

It is also closed under multiplication on the right by $s^{-1}$: put $r' = \overline{rs^{-1}}$.
Then $r's$ lies in the coset $Hr$, so $\overline{r's} = r$ and $r'sr^{-1} \in U$, and

$$krs^{-1} = k\,\bigl(r'sr^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}\,r'.$$

Since every element of $G$ is a product of elements of $S$ and their inverses, $KR = G$.

Finally let $h \in H$ and write $h = kr$. Then $r = k^{-1}h$ lies in $H$, the coset $H \cdot 1$.
That coset meets $R$ only in $1$, so $r = 1$ and $h = k \in K$. Hence $K = H$.

## Related concepts

The [Nielsen–Schreier theorem](trough:Subgroup.isFreeGroupOfIsFreeGroup) states that
every subgroup of a free group is free. When $G$ is
free on $S$ and the transversal is chosen more carefully, as a so-called Schreier transversal,
the nontrivial Schreier generators form a free basis of $H$. This leads to the Schreier index formula: a
subgroup of index $n$ in a free group of rank $k$ is free of rank $n(k-1) + 1$, as in the free
group example above, where $n = k = 2$ gives rank $3$. In finite group theory the Schreier
generators of a point stabilizer in a permutation group underlie the Schreier–Sims
algorithm.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib states the lemma for an arbitrary group, with no finiteness assumptions on the
generating set, the subgroup or its index. The transversal is expressed through Mathlib's
general notion of complementary subsets, where every element factors uniquely as an element of
the subgroup times an element of the transversal, and the chosen representative of each element
is a function supplied by that complement. The main statement describes the subgroup generated
inside the ambient group; a companion statement says
the same set generates the subgroup regarded as a group in its own right, and
a third version works with finite sets. Each of these
has an additive counterpart for additive groups. The counting
consequences assume the subgroup has finite index, which matters because Mathlib assigns index
$0$ to a subgroup of infinite index. The rank of a group is defined only for finitely generated
groups, as the least size of a finite generating set.
