A **presented group** is a [group](trough:Group) described by a set of generators together with a set of relations that those generators must satisfy, and by nothing else. Writing $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ for the group with generators $S$ and relations $R$ gives a compact, finite description of many infinite groups, and it is the standard way groups arise in combinatorial group theory and topology, for instance as fundamental groups of spaces built from cells. Every group has a presentation, so the notion is fully general; the price is that a presentation can hide basic facts about its group, such as whether two words name the same element.

## Definition

Let $S$ be a set and let $F(S)$ be the [free group](trough:FreeGroup) on $S$, whose elements are reduced words in the letters of $S$ and their inverses. A set $R \subseteq F(S)$ of words is called a set of relators. Let $N$ be the smallest normal subgroup of $F(S)$ containing $R$, its normal closure, which is the subgroup generated by all conjugates $w r w^{-1}$ with $r \in R$ and $w \in F(S)$. The presented group is the [quotient](trough:QuotientGroup.Quotient.group)

$$\langle S \mid R \rangle = F(S)/N,$$

which is the group with generators $S$ and relators $R$.

A relation written as an equation $u = v$ between two words stands for the relator $u v^{-1}$; the two descriptions give the same group. Each generator $s \in S$ has an image in the quotient, and these images generate the whole group. Every relator becomes the identity, and more precisely a word represents the identity exactly when it lies in the normal closure $N$: the relators and their consequences are the only identities imposed.

A group $G$ has the presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ when $G$ is isomorphic to it. A presentation is finite when $S$ and $R$ are both finite, and a group admitting a finite presentation is called finitely presented.

## Examples

- **Free groups.** With no relators, $\langle S \mid \varnothing \rangle$ is the free group $F(S)$ itself.
- **Cyclic groups.** For $n \ge 1$, the group $\langle a \mid a^n \rangle$ is cyclic of order $n$, and $\langle a \mid \varnothing \rangle$ is the infinite cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}$ (see [cyclic group](trough:IsCyclic)).
- **Free abelian group of rank two.** The single relator $a b a^{-1} b^{-1}$ forces the two generators to commute, so $\langle a, b \mid a b a^{-1} b^{-1} \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$.
- **Dihedral groups.** The symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon has the presentation $\langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, (s r)^2 \rangle$, with $r$ a rotation and $s$ a reflection; it has order $2n$.
- **Coxeter groups.** Given a symmetric matrix $(m_{ij})$ of positive integers with $m_{ii} = 1$ and $m_{ij} \ge 2$ for $i \ne j$, the group $\langle s_i \mid (s_i s_j)^{m_{ij}} \rangle$ is the Coxeter group of the matrix; Mathlib also allows the entry $0$, meaning that no relation is imposed on $s_i s_j$. Finite reflection groups and the symmetric groups arise this way.

## Properties

**Universal property.** Let $G$ be a group and $f \colon S \to G$ a function such that every relator evaluates to the identity when each letter $s$ is replaced by $f(s)$. Then $f$ extends to a group homomorphism $\langle S \mid R \rangle \to G$ that sends each generator $s$ to $f(s)$, and this extension is the only homomorphism that does so. In words, a homomorphism out of a presented group is the same as an assignment of elements to the generators that respects the relations. This is the main tool for working with presentations: to define a [group morphism](trough:MonoidHom) one only needs to check that each relator is sent to the identity (finitely many checks when the presentation is finite). To prove that a group $G$ has a given presentation, one typically uses this property to build a homomorphism onto $G$ and then shows that it is injective, or builds an inverse by other means.

**Every group is presented.** If $G$ is any group, take $S$ to be a generating set, map $F(S)$ onto $G$, and take $R$ to be the kernel of this map. By the [first isomorphism theorem](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange) $G \cong \langle S \mid R \rangle$. More generally, if a family of elements generates $G$ and a set of words normally generates the kernel of the induced map from the free group onto $G$, then $G$ is isomorphic to the group presented by those generators and words. In particular every finitely presented group is isomorphic to a presented group with finitely many generators and relators.

**Constructions.** Renaming the generators along a bijection, and the relators with them, gives an isomorphic presented group. The free product of $\langle S_1 \mid R_1 \rangle$ and $\langle S_2 \mid R_2 \rangle$ is presented by the disjoint union of the generators and the union of the relators. Adding relators to a presentation gives a quotient of the original group.

**Algorithmic questions.** A presentation determines its group, but extracting information from it can be impossible in general. There are finitely presented groups in which no algorithm decides whether a given word represents the identity, and no algorithm decides, given finite presentations, whether two of them define isomorphic groups or whether one defines the trivial group. Some invariants are computable: the [abelianization](trough:Abelianization) can be read off a finite presentation by linear algebra over $\mathbb{Z}$ (Smith normal form), but it forgets most of the group.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib builds the presented group from a type of generators, which may be infinite, and an arbitrary set of elements of the free group on that type, read as relators rather than as equations. The group is defined directly as the quotient of the free group by the normal closure of the relators, so it is a concrete group rather than a property of some other group; the statement that a given group has a presentation is recorded separately, as a structure carrying the generating family, the relators and a proof that the relators normally generate the kernel. Finite presentability is a property of a group, phrased through a surjection from a free group on finitely many generators whose kernel is the normal closure of a finite set. The construction itself has a counterpart for additive groups, the presented additive group.
