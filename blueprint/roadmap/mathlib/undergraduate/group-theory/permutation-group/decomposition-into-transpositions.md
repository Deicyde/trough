# Decomposition into transpositions

The **decomposition into transpositions** expresses every permutation of a finite set as a
product of transpositions, permutations that exchange two elements and fix all the others.
It shows that the transpositions generate the [symmetric group](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/permutation-group-of-a-type.md), which makes
them the basic building blocks for computing with permutations and for proving facts about
all of them at once. The decomposition is far from unique, but the parity of the number of
factors is determined by the permutation: it is recorded by the
[signature](signature.md), which equals $(-1)^m$ for any decomposition into $m$
transpositions.

## Statement

Let $X$ be a finite set. A
[transposition of $X$ is a permutation that exchanges two distinct elements $a \neq b$ and fixes every other element](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Support.html#Equiv.Perm.IsSwap);
it is written $(a\ b)$. Then
[every permutation $\sigma$ of $X$ is a product of transpositions](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.truncSwapFactors):

$$\sigma = \tau_1 \tau_2 \cdots \tau_m,$$

where each $\tau_i$ is a transposition and products are compositions, so that $\tau_m$ is
applied first. The identity is the empty product, with $m = 0$. If $X$ has $n \ge 1$
elements, the factors can be chosen so that $m \le n - 1$.

## Examples

- A cycle of length $k$ is a product of $k - 1$ transpositions. One choice is
  $$(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k) = (a_1\ a_2)(a_2\ a_3)\cdots(a_{k-1}\ a_k),$$
  which can be checked by following each $a_i$ through the factors from right to left.
- In the symmetric group on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, the cycle $(1\ 2\ 3)$ equals $(1\ 2)(2\ 3)$, and
  also $(1\ 3)(1\ 2)$ and $(2\ 3)(1\ 3)$. Since
  [a transposition is its own inverse](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/End.html#Equiv.swap_inv), one can also insert a
  pair $(a\ b)(a\ b)$ anywhere, so decompositions of any length $m + 2j$ exist once one of
  length $m$ does.
- On $\{1, \dots, 6\}$, the permutation $(1\ 4\ 2)(3\ 6)$, written as a product of
  [disjoint cycles](decomposition-into-cycles-with-disjoint-support.md), equals $(1\ 4)(4\ 2)(3\ 6)$.

## Proof

The proof is by induction on the number of points that $\sigma$ moves. If $\sigma$ moves no
point, it is the identity and the empty product works. Otherwise pick $x$ with
$\sigma(x) \neq x$ and let $\tau = (x\ \sigma(x))$. The permutation $\tau\sigma$ fixes $x$.
It also fixes every point $y$ fixed by $\sigma$: such a $y$ differs from $x$, and it differs
from $\sigma(x)$ because $\sigma(y) = y$ and $\sigma$ is injective, so $\tau$ fixes $y$. Hence
$\tau\sigma$ moves fewer points than $\sigma$, and by induction it is a product
$\tau_1 \cdots \tau_m$ of transpositions. Since $\tau^2$ is the identity,
$\sigma = \tau \tau_1 \cdots \tau_m$. Each step removes at least one moved point, and no
permutation moves exactly one point, so a permutation moving $s \ge 2$ points needs at most
$s - 1$ steps. Since $s \le n$, this gives the bound $m \le n - 1$ from the statement.

Alternatively, one can write $\sigma$ as a product of disjoint cycles and decompose each cycle
as in the first example.

## Consequences

- **Parity.** By multiplicativity of the sign,
  [a product of $m$ transpositions has sign $(-1)^m$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.sign_prod_list_swap).
  So all decompositions of a given permutation have lengths of the same parity, and the
  permutation is even or odd accordingly. In particular
  [a cycle moving $k$ points has sign $-(-1)^k = (-1)^{k-1}$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Cycle/Basic.html#Equiv.Perm.IsCycle.sign).
  The even permutations form the [alternating group](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-02/alternating-group.md).
- **Generation.** For a finite set $X$,
  [the transpositions generate the group of permutations of $X$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.closure_isSwap)
  (see [subgroup generated by a subset](../../../overview/general-algebra/group-theory/part-01/subgroup-generated-by-a-subset.md)). Smaller generating sets
  follow by expressing transpositions in terms of others:
  [every permutation of $\{0, 1, \dots, k\}$ is a product of transpositions $(i\ \ i+1)$ of consecutive elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.mclosure_swap_castSucc_succ),
  and [a cycle $\sigma$ moving every point of $X$ together with a transposition $(x\ \sigma(x))$ generate the whole group](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Closure.html#Equiv.Perm.closure_cycle_adjacent_swap).
  When $X$ has a prime number of elements,
  [a cycle moving every point together with any transposition generate it](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Closure.html#Equiv.Perm.closure_prime_cycle_swap).
- **Induction over permutations.** For a finite set, a property that holds for the identity
  and is preserved by composing on the left with a transposition
  [holds for every permutation](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.swap_induction_on).
- **Uniqueness of the sign.** Any two transpositions are conjugate, so a homomorphism to
  $\{\pm 1\}$ takes the same value on all of them and is determined by that value. Hence
  [every surjective homomorphism from the permutations of a finite set onto $\{\pm 1\}$ is the sign](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.eq_sign_of_surjective_hom).
- **Minimal length.** If $X$ has $n$ elements, the fewest transpositions needed for $\sigma$ is $n - c$, where $c$ is
  the number of cycles of $\sigma$, fixed points counted as cycles of length $1$.

## Infinite sets

On an infinite set $X$ a product of finitely many transpositions moves only finitely many
points, so a permutation moving infinitely many points, such as the shift $k \mapsto k + 1$ of the integers, is not such a product. Because each transposition is its own
inverse, the subgroup generated by the transpositions consists exactly of their finite
products, and a permutation of an arbitrary set
[lies in this subgroup if and only if it moves only finitely many points](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/ClosureSwap.html#mem_closure_isSwap').

## Related concepts

The [decomposition into disjoint cycles](decomposition-into-cycles-with-disjoint-support.md) is a sharper
statement: it is unique up to the order of the factors, once cycles of length $1$ are left out. An analogue in linear algebra is the
[decomposition of an orthogonal transformation as a product of reflections](../../bilinear-and-quadratic-forms-over-a-vector-space/endomorphisms/decomposition-of-an-orthogonal-transformation-as.md),
in which reflections play the role of transpositions.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib states the theorem for a finite type with decidable equality, as a list of
permutations whose product is the given permutation and each of whose entries exchanges two
distinct elements; the product of a list composes its entries so that the last entry acts
first. The result is packaged as a truncation: it computably provides such a list, while
treating all such lists as indistinguishable, since the list is not unique. The bare
existence statement used in this article is a consequence. When the type is also linearly
ordered, Mathlib defines
[a specific list of transpositions with the given product](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Sign.html#Equiv.Perm.swapFactors),
obtained by running the procedure of the proof above through the elements in increasing
order. The generation statements are phrased with the subgroup or submonoid generated by the
given set of permutations, and in the statement for arbitrary types the set of moved points
is written as the complement of the set of fixed points.
