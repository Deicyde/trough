# Classification of abelian simple groups

The **classification of abelian simple groups** says that an abelian
[group](../part-01/group.md) is simple exactly when its order is a prime number, so that
up to isomorphism the abelian simple groups are the cyclic groups
$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, one for each prime $p$. Finite simple groups are the
building blocks from which every finite group is assembled by successive
extensions, as the factors of a composition series, and this theorem
settles the commutative case completely: every other simple group is
nonabelian, which is where the difficulty of the
classification of finite simple groups lies.

## Statement

A group $G$ is [simple](../part-01/simple-group.md) if it is nontrivial and its only
[normal subgroups](../part-01/normal-subgroup.md) are the trivial subgroup and $G$
itself ([the definition](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Subgroup/Simple.html#IsSimpleGroup)). The theorem reads:

Let $G$ be an abelian group. Then
[$G$ is simple if and only if its order is a prime number](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#CommGroup.is_simple_iff_prime_card).

In particular an abelian simple group is automatically finite, and it is
[cyclic](../part-01/cyclic-group.md), generated by any element other than the identity.

## Examples

- For each prime $p$, the integers modulo $p$ under addition form a simple
  group: [the additive group of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is simple](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#ZMod.instIsSimpleAddGroup).
- The group $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian but not simple, since the
  multiples of $2$ form a subgroup of order $3$, which is normal because the
  group is abelian.
- The [integers](../../numbers/integer.md) under addition are not simple: the even integers form a
  proper nontrivial subgroup. Likewise the rationals contain the integers as a
  proper nontrivial subgroup. No infinite abelian group is simple.
- Simple groups that are not abelian exist, for instance the
  [alternating groups](alternating-group.md) $A_n$, which
  [are simple for $n \ge 5$](alternating-groups-on-5-letters-or-more-are-simp.md). The group
  $A_5$, of order $60$, is the smallest nonabelian simple group.

## Proof

One direction needs no commutativity at all. If $|G| = p$ is prime, then by
[Lagrange's theorem](lagranges-theorem.md)
[the order of every subgroup divides $p$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Coset/Card.html#Subgroup.card_subgroup_dvd_card),
so it is $1$ or $p$, and
[every subgroup is either trivial or all of $G$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#Subgroup.eq_bot_or_eq_top_of_prime_card).
Since $p > 1$ the group is nontrivial, hence
[every group of prime order is simple](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#isSimpleGroup_of_prime_card).

For the converse, let $G$ be abelian and simple. In an abelian group every
subgroup is normal, so simplicity says that $G$ has no subgroups other than
$\{e\}$ and $G$. Choose an element $g \neq e$. The cyclic subgroup it generates
is not trivial, so it is all of $G$, and $G$ is cyclic. If $g$ had infinite
[order](../../../../undergraduate/group-theory/basic-definitions/order-of-an-element.md), the subgroup generated by $g^2$ would be proper and
nontrivial, which is impossible; so $G$ is finite of some order $n > 1$. If $n$
factored as $n = ab$ with $1 < a < n$, the subgroup generated by $g^a$ would have
order $b$, strictly between $1$ and $n$, again impossible. Hence $n$ is prime:
[an abelian simple group has prime order](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsSimpleGroup.prime_card), and in
particular [it is finite](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsSimpleGroup.finite).

## Consequences

- **Groups of prime order.** Combining the two directions, a finite group of
  prime order is simple and
  [cyclic](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic/Subgroup.html#isCyclic_of_prime_card), hence abelian, so the groups of prime
  order are precisely the abelian simple groups.
- **Composition factors.** By the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](../../../../thousand-plus/algebra/20-group-theory-and-generalizations/jordan-holder-theorem.md)
  every finite group has simple composition factors that are determined by the
  group up to isomorphism and order. The
  theorem identifies which of them can be abelian: exactly the cyclic groups of
  prime order. A finite group is solvable precisely when all of its composition
  factors are of this kind.
- **Odd order.** The Feit–Thompson theorem says that every finite
  group of odd order is solvable. Together with this classification, it implies
  that a finite simple group of odd order is cyclic of prime order.

## Related concepts

The cyclic groups of prime order, which this theorem identifies, form the
abelian, and easiest, family in the classification of finite simple groups,
whose other members (the alternating groups, the groups of Lie type and the
sporadic groups) are all nonabelian. More generally, the
[structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups](structure-of-finitely-generated-abelian-groups.md)
describes every finitely generated abelian group, not only the simple ones, as a
product of cyclic groups. The [abelianization](../part-01/abelianization.md) of a group
is its largest abelian quotient; a nonabelian simple group has trivial
abelianization.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib measures the order of a group with a cardinality that is $0$ for an
infinite set. Since $0$ is not prime, the statement that an abelian group is
simple exactly when its order is prime already contains the fact that no
infinite abelian group is simple, and Mathlib
[records that consequence separately](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#IsSimpleGroup.finite). Simplicity is a property of a group rather than extra structure, and
the definition includes nontriviality, so the trivial group is not simple. The
theorem is stated for groups with a bundled commutative structure; a companion
version asks only that the multiplication of an ordinary group be commutative
([the same equivalence](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#Group.is_simple_iff_prime_card)), and the direction
from prime order to simplicity holds for arbitrary groups. The additive form,
for groups written with $+$, is
[stated as well](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/SpecificGroups/Cyclic.html#AddCommGroup.is_simple_iff_prime_card).
