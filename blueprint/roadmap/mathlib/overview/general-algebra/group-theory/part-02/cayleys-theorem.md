# Cayley's theorem

**Cayley's theorem** says that every [group](../part-01/group.md) is isomorphic to a
group of permutations: a group $G$ can be realized inside the
[permutation group](permutation-group-of-a-type.md) of the set $G$ itself, by letting each
element act on the others through multiplication. The theorem shows that the
abstract axioms of a group describe nothing more general than groups of
symmetries of a set. It also reduces the study of finite
groups, in principle, to subgroups of the symmetric groups $S_n$.

## Statement

For a set $X$, write $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ for the group of all bijections
$X \to X$ under composition. Every group $G$ is
[isomorphic to a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(G)$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Perm/Subgroup.html#Equiv.Perm.subgroupOfMulAction).
The subgroup is the image of the map $g \mapsto \lambda_g$, where
$\lambda_g(x) = gx$ is left multiplication by $g$. This embedding is called the
left regular representation of $G$.

If $G$ is finite of order $n$, then numbering its elements makes
$\operatorname{Sym}(G)$ a copy of the symmetric group
$S_n = \operatorname{Sym}(\{1, \dots, n\})$, which
[has $n!$ elements](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Data/Finite/Perm.html#Nat.card_perm). So every group of order $n$ is
isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_n$. In particular there are only finitely many
groups of order $n$ up to isomorphism, since $S_n$ has only finitely many
subgroups.

## Proof

Each $\lambda_g$ is a bijection of $G$, with inverse $\lambda_{g^{-1}}$. The
associative law gives $\lambda_{gh}(x) = ghx = \lambda_g(\lambda_h(x))$, so
$g \mapsto \lambda_g$ is a [group homomorphism](../part-01/group-morphism.md) from $G$ to
$\operatorname{Sym}(G)$. It is injective, because $\lambda_g(e) = g$ recovers
$g$ from $\lambda_g$, where $e$ is the identity. An injective homomorphism
[maps its domain isomorphically onto its image](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/Subgroup/Ker.html#MonoidHom.ofInjective),
and the image of a homomorphism is a [subgroup](../part-01/subgroup.md).

The same argument works for any [group action](../part-01/group-action.md) of $G$ on a
set $X$. Each element $g$ moves the points of $X$ by a permutation, and
[these permutations multiply as the elements of $G$ do](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/Action/End.html#MulAction.toPermHom).
The action is called faithful when only the identity fixes every point, that
is, when distinct elements act differently; for a faithful action
[the map from $G$ to $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ is injective](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/Action/Basic.html#MulAction.toPerm_injective),
so $G$ is isomorphic to its image. Left multiplication is a faithful action of
$G$ on itself.

## Examples

- **Cyclic groups.** In the cyclic group of order $n$ generated by $a$,
  multiplication by $a$ sends $a^k$ to $a^{k+1}$, an $n$-cycle on the $n$
  elements. The group is realized as the subgroup of $S_n$ generated by one
  $n$-cycle.
- **The Klein four-group.** Its elements are $e, a, b, c$, where each of
  $a, b, c$ has order $2$ and the product of any two distinct ones is the third.
  Multiplication by $a$ swaps $e$ with $a$ and $b$ with $c$. Numbering the
  elements $1, 2, 3, 4$ gives the subgroup
  $\{\mathrm{id}, (1\,2)(3\,4), (1\,3)(2\,4), (1\,4)(2\,3)\}$ of $S_4$.
- **Degree of the embedding.** The theorem places $S_3$ inside $S_6$, though $S_3$
  already acts faithfully on three points. The degree $n = |G|$ cannot always
  be lowered. The quaternion group $\{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, with
  $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$, has order $8$, and every nontrivial subgroup of
  it contains $-1$. In a faithful action only the identity fixes every point, so
  the stabilizers cannot all contain $-1$; some point has trivial stabilizer,
  and its orbit has $8$ points.

## Consequences

Letting $G$ act on the left cosets of a subgroup, rather than on its elements,
gives a refinement. Let $H$ be a subgroup of finite [index](../part-01/index-of-a-subgroup.md)
$n$. The action of $G$ on the $n$ cosets $gH$ gives a homomorphism
$G \to \operatorname{Sym}(G/H) \cong S_n$, usually not injective, whose kernel is
[the normal core of $H$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/GroupAction/Quotient.html#Subgroup.normalCore_eq_ker): the elements of $G$ all
of whose conjugates lie in $H$. The kernel is a [normal subgroup](../part-01/normal-subgroup.md)
contained in $H$, and the quotient by it embeds in $S_n$, so
[the index of the normal core divides $n!$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/IndexNormal.html#Subgroup.index_normalCore_dvd_factorial_index).

- For $n = 2$ the index of the normal core is a multiple of $2$ (the core lies
  in $H$) and divides $2! = 2$,
  so $H$ equals its core: [every subgroup of index $2$ is normal](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/IndexNormal.html#Subgroup.normal_of_index_eq_two).
- More generally, in a finite group,
  [a subgroup whose index is the smallest prime $p$ dividing $|G|$ is normal](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/IndexNormal.html#Subgroup.normal_of_index_eq_minFac_card):
  the index of the normal core divides both $|G|$ and $p!$; since no prime
  smaller than $p$ divides $|G|$ and $p^2$ does not divide $p!$, it divides $p$.
  It is also a multiple of $p$, because the core lies in $H$, so it equals $p$
  and $H$ equals its core.
- If $G$ is [simple](../part-01/simple-group.md) and has a subgroup of finite index
  $n > 1$, the normal core is trivial, so $G$ embeds in $S_n$ and $|G|$ divides $n!$.

Combining the left regular representation with the [signature](../../../../undergraduate/group-theory/permutation-group/signature.md) of
a permutation gives another classical consequence: a group of order $2m$ with
$m$ odd has a normal subgroup of index $2$. An element of order $2$, which
exists by [Cauchy's theorem](../../../../thousand-plus/algebra/20-group-theory-and-generalizations/part-01/cauchys-theorem-group-theory.md), has no
fixed points under left multiplication, so it permutes $G$ as a product of $m$
disjoint transpositions, an odd permutation. The elements of $G$ that act by even
permutations then form a normal subgroup of index $2$.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib proves the theorem in the generality of the second paragraph of the
proof: for any faithful action of a group $G$ on a type $X$, it gives an
isomorphism between $G$ and the image of the homomorphism from $G$ to
the permutations of $X$. The classical statement is the case where $X$ is $G$
acting on itself by left multiplication; Mathlib finds the faithfulness of that
action automatically. The isomorphism is constructed with the axiom of choice
and is not computable. In the smallest-prime criterion, Mathlib's cardinality assigns $0$ to an
infinite group, and the smallest prime factor of $0$ is taken to be $2$, so for
an infinite group the statement reduces to the index-$2$ case and needs no
finiteness hypothesis.
