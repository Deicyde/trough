The **exterior algebra** of a [module](trough:Module) $M$ over a commutative ring $R$, written
$\Lambda(M)$, is the [associative algebra](trough:Algebra) generated by $M$ subject only to the rule
that every element of $M$ squares to zero. Its product, the wedge product $\wedge$, is the algebraic
form of oriented area and volume: in an $n$-dimensional space with basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$, the wedge
of $n$ vectors is their [determinant](trough:Module.Basis.det) in that basis times
$e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. The exterior algebra underlies the theory of
determinants, alternating multilinear forms and differential forms, and it is the simplest case of a
[Clifford algebra](trough:CliffordAlgebra).

## Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $M$ an $R$-module. The
exterior algebra of $M$ is the quotient of the
[tensor algebra](trough:TensorAlgebra) $T(M)$ by the two-sided ideal generated by the elements
$x \otimes x$ for $x \in M$. It comes with an $R$-linear map $\iota : M \to \Lambda(M)$, and
$\iota$ is injective, so $M$ is usually identified with its image. The
product in $\Lambda(M)$ is written $\wedge$, and by construction
$x \wedge x = 0$ for every $x \in M$.

Equivalently, $\Lambda(M)$ is characterized by a universal property. Let $A$ be an associative
$R$-algebra with identity and $f : M \to A$ a [linear map](trough:LinearMap) with $f(x)^2 = 0$ for all
$x \in M$. Then there is exactly one homomorphism of $R$-algebras $\bar f : \Lambda(M) \to A$ with
$\bar f \circ \iota = f$: composing with $\iota$ gives a
bijection between algebra homomorphisms $\Lambda(M) \to A$ and linear maps $M \to A$ whose values square to zero,
and an algebra homomorphism $g$ satisfies $g \circ \iota = f$ exactly when $g = \bar f$.
The canonical map from $T(M)$ is the
algebra homomorphism that is the identity on $M$.

For $n \ge 0$ the $n$-th exterior power $\Lambda^n(M)$ is the
submodule of $\Lambda(M)$ consisting of the $R$-linear combinations of products of $n$ elements of
$M$ (for $n = 0$, the multiples of $1$); it is
spanned by the products $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_n$.
Here $\Lambda^0(M)$ is the image of $R$ (a copy of $R$, since $R \to \Lambda(M)$ is injective) and
$\Lambda^1(M)$ is the image of $M$.

## Examples

- If $M = R$, then $\Lambda(R) = R \oplus R\,e$ with $e = \iota(1)$ and $e \wedge e = 0$: the ring of
  dual numbers $R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$.
- If $M = R^2$ with standard basis $e_1, e_2$, then $\Lambda(M)$ is free on $1, e_1, e_2, e_1 \wedge e_2$,
  and for vectors $u = a e_1 + c e_2$ and $v = b e_1 + d e_2$,

  $$u \wedge v = ad\, e_1 \wedge e_2 + cb\, e_2 \wedge e_1 = (ad - bc)\, e_1 \wedge e_2,$$

  the determinant of the matrix with columns $u$ and $v$ times $e_1 \wedge e_2$.
- If $M = R^3$, the coefficients of $u \wedge v$ on $e_2 \wedge e_3$, $e_3 \wedge e_1$ and
  $e_1 \wedge e_2$ are the components of the cross product $u \times v$, and $u \wedge v \wedge w$ is
  the determinant of $u, v, w$ times $e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$. For $R$ nonzero the exterior
  powers are free of ranks $1, 3, 3, 1$.

## Properties

- **Anticommutativity.** Expanding $(x + y) \wedge (x + y) = 0$ gives
  $x \wedge y + y \wedge x = 0$ for all $x, y \in M$. Imposing
  only the relations $x \wedge y + y \wedge x = 0$ does not force $x \wedge x = 0$ in general when $2$
  is not invertible in $R$: over a field of characteristic $2$ those relations give the symmetric
  algebra of a nonzero $M$, a polynomial ring, in which $x^2 \neq 0$ for $x \neq 0$.
- **Alternating maps.** The map $(m_1, \dots, m_n) \mapsto m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_n$ is
  multilinear and alternating: it
  vanishes whenever two of the $m_i$ are equal.
  It is universal for this property: for every $R$-module $N$,
  linear maps $\Lambda(M) \to N$ correspond to families, one for each $n$, of alternating $n$-linear maps from $M$ to $N$.
  (See [alternating map](trough:AlternatingMap).)
- **Grading.** $\Lambda(M)$ is
  the internal direct sum of the exterior powers $\Lambda^n(M)$, with degrees adding under multiplication.
  It is graded commutative: if $\alpha$ is a product of $i$ elements of $M$ and $\beta$ a product of
  $j$ elements, then $\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{ij}\, \beta \wedge \alpha$.
- **Bases and ranks.** Let $M$ be free with a [basis](trough:Module.Basis) $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, and
  order $I$ totally. For a finite subset $S = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\}$ put
  $e_S = e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}$, with $e_\varnothing = 1$. Then
  the $e_S$ form a basis of $\Lambda(M)$, and
  the $e_S$ with $|S| = n$ form a basis of $\Lambda^n(M)$. If $S$
  and $T$ share an element, $e_S \wedge e_T = 0$.
  Consequently, if $R$ is nonzero and $M$ is free of finite rank $r$, then
  $\Lambda^n(M)$ has rank $\binom{r}{n}$, so $\Lambda^n(M) = 0$ for
  $n > r$ and $\Lambda(M)$ has rank $2^r$.
- **Functoriality.** A linear map $f : M \to N$ induces
  an algebra homomorphism $\Lambda(f) : \Lambda(M) \to \Lambda(N)$ extending $f$,
  with $\Lambda(f)(m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_n) = f(m_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge f(m_n)$ and
  $\Lambda(g) \circ \Lambda(f) = \Lambda(g \circ f)$.
  $\Lambda(f)$ is surjective exactly when $f$ is, and for
  vector spaces over a field,
  $\Lambda(f)$ is injective when $f$ is.
- **Determinants.** If $V$ is a vector space of dimension $n$, then $\Lambda^n(V)$ is one-dimensional,
  so an endomorphism $T$ of $V$ acts on it as multiplication by a scalar; that scalar is the
  [determinant](trough:LinearMap.det) of $T$. Similarly, $n$ vectors form a basis of $V$ exactly when
  their wedge is nonzero; more generally, vectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ in a vector space are linearly
  independent exactly when $v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \neq 0$.

## Related concepts

The [Clifford algebra](trough:CliffordAlgebra) of a [quadratic form](trough:QuadraticForm) $Q$ on $M$
is the quotient of $T(M)$ by the relations $x \otimes x = Q(x)$; the exterior algebra is the case
$Q = 0$. Replacing $x \otimes x$ by the relations $x \otimes y = y \otimes x$ gives the symmetric
algebra instead, a commutative analogue whose degree $n$ part is spanned by the products $m_1 \cdots m_n$,
now symmetric rather than alternating in their factors. In geometry, the differential forms on a smooth manifold are built pointwise
from the exterior algebra of the dual of each tangent space.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines the exterior algebra of a module over any commutative ring, with no freeness or
finiteness assumption on the module, as the Clifford algebra of the zero quadratic form, so that the
universal property, the induction principle and functoriality are inherited from the Clifford algebra.
The $n$-th exterior power is not a separate module but a submodule of the exterior algebra: the $n$-th
power, in the sense of products of submodules, of the image of $M$. The universal property is packaged
as a bijection between linear maps whose values square to zero and algebra homomorphisms into any
associative algebra, commutative or not. The bases require a total order on the index set, used to
list each finite subset in increasing order, and the rank formula assumes a nonzero ring and a free
module of finite rank.
