# Quotient ring

A **quotient ring** is the ring obtained from a [ring](../rings/ring.md) $R$ by
declaring the elements of a two-sided [ideal](ideal-of-a-commutative-ring.md) $I$ (any ideal, when
$R$ is commutative) to be zero. Its elements
are the cosets $a + I$, added and multiplied through representatives. Quotient
rings are the ring-theoretic counterpart of [quotient groups](../group-theory/part-01/quotient-group.md):
they turn congruence modulo an ideal into equality, they describe the image of
every [ring morphism](../rings/ring-morphism.md), and they are the standard way to build new
rings, such as the integers modulo $n$ or fields containing a root of a given
polynomial.

## Definition

Let $R$ be a ring and $I$ a two-sided ideal of $R$, that is, an additive subgroup
with $ra \in I$ and $ar \in I$ for all $a \in I$ and $r \in R$. As a set, $R/I$
is the set of cosets $a + I$ of $I$ in the additive group of $R$, and the
operations are

$$(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I, \qquad (a + I)(b + I) = ab + I.$$

These do not depend on the chosen representatives. Addition is handled by the
quotient group construction. For multiplication, if $a - a' \in I$ and
$b - b' \in I$, then

$$ab - a'b' = a(b - b') + (a - a')b',$$

and both terms lie in $I$, the first because $I$ absorbs multiplication on the
left and the second because it absorbs multiplication on the right. The ring
axioms then pass from $R$ to the cosets, so
[the quotient of $R$ by a two-sided ideal is a ring](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.ring),
with zero $0 + I = I$ and identity $1 + I$. If $R$ is commutative, every ideal is
two-sided and [$R/I$ is again commutative](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.commRing).

[The quotient map $a \mapsto a + I$ is a ring morphism](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.mk),
and [it is surjective](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.mk_surjective). Two elements have the
same image [exactly when their difference lies in $I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.eq),
so [the kernel of the quotient map is $I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Operations.html#Ideal.mk_ker). Together with the
fact that kernels of ring morphisms are two-sided ideals, this shows that the
two-sided ideals of $R$ are exactly the kernels of ring morphisms out of $R$.

## Examples

- For a natural number $n$, the quotient of the [integers](../numbers/integer.md) by the
  ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$ is
  [the ring of integers modulo $n$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Data/ZMod/QuotientRing.html#Int.quotientSpanNatEquivZMod). For
  $n = 0$ this is $\mathbb{Z}$ itself, and for $n = 1$ it is the zero ring.
- For a commutative ring $R$ and $c \in R$, evaluation at $c$ identifies the
  quotient of the [polynomial ring](../polynomials-and-power-series/polynomial-in-one-indeterminate.md) $R[x]$ by the ideal
  generated by $x - c$ with
  [$R$ itself](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Quotient.html#Polynomial.quotientSpanXSubCAlgEquiv).
- The quotient $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$ is isomorphic to the
  [complex numbers](../numbers/complex-number.md): the morphism $\mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$
  sending $x$ to $i$ is surjective with kernel $(x^2 + 1)$. More generally,
  for a commutative ring $R$ and a polynomial $f \in R[x]$, the ring obtained by
  [adjoining a root of $f$ to $R$](../field-theory/rupture-field.md) is
  [defined as the quotient $R[x]/(f)$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/AdjoinRoot.html#AdjoinRoot).
- The quotient by the zero ideal is a copy of $R$, while
  [$0 = 1$ in $R/I$ exactly when $I = R$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Basic.html#Ideal.Quotient.zero_eq_one_iff),
  so the quotient by $R$ itself is the zero ring.

## Properties

**Universal property.** Let $f\colon R \to S$ be a ring morphism with
$f(a) = 0$ for every $a \in I$. Then $f$
[induces a ring morphism $\bar f\colon R/I \to S$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.lift)
with [$\bar f(a + I) = f(a)$ for all $a \in R$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.lift_mk),
and since every element of $R/I$ is a coset,
[a ring morphism out of $R/I$ is determined by its composite with the quotient map](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.ringHom_ext).
Applied with $I = \ker f$, the induced map is also injective, since
$\bar f(a + I) = 0$ means $f(a) = 0$, that is, $a \in I$ and $a + I = I$. So for a surjective
ring morphism $f\colon R \to S$,
[$R/\ker f$ is isomorphic to $S$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Operations.html#RingHom.quotientKerEquivOfSurjective);
this is the [first isomorphism theorem](first-isomorphism-theorem-for-commutative-rings.md).

**Prime and maximal ideals.** Let $R$ be commutative. Then
[$R/I$ is an integral domain exactly when $I$ is a prime ideal](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Basic.html#Ideal.Quotient.isDomain_iff_prime),
and [$I$ is a maximal ideal exactly when $R/I$ is a field](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Basic.html#Ideal.Quotient.maximal_ideal_iff_isField_quotient)
(see [prime ideal](prime-ideal.md), [maximal ideal](maximal-ideal.md)
and [field](../field-theory/field.md)). For instance $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field for
every prime $p$, while $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not even an integral domain,
since $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ there. Since every field is an integral domain,
every maximal ideal is prime.

**Ideals of a quotient.** Let $R$ be commutative. For a surjective ring morphism
$f\colon R \to S$,
[the ideals of $S$ correspond to the ideals of $R$ containing $\ker f$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Maps.html#Ideal.relIsoOfSurjective),
by taking preimages, and the correspondence preserves inclusion. Applied to the
quotient map, the ideals of $R/I$ are the images of the ideals of $R$ that
contain $I$. For ideals $I \subseteq J$ of $R$, the image $J/I$ of
$J$ in $R/I$ satisfies
[$(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Operations.html#DoubleQuot.quotQuotEquivQuotOfLE).

**Products.** The [Chinese remainder theorem](chinese-remainder-theorem.md)
states that for finitely many ideals $I_1, \dots, I_k$ of a commutative ring
with $I_i + I_j = R$ whenever $i \neq j$,
[the quotient by $I_1 \cap \dots \cap I_k$ is isomorphic to the product of the quotients $R/I_j$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Operations.html#Ideal.quotientInfRingEquivPiQuotient).
For coprime integers $m$ and $n$ this gives
$\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines an ideal of a possibly noncommutative ring as a left ideal, a
subset containing $0$ and closed under addition and under multiplication on the left, and treats
being two-sided as a separate condition. The quotient $R/I$ is first built as a
set by reusing the quotient of a module by a submodule, viewing $R$ as a module
over itself; its ring structure is then supplied whenever the ideal is
two-sided, which is automatic for commutative rings. No properness is assumed,
so the quotient by the whole ring is allowed and is the zero ring. The field
structure on the quotient of a commutative ring by a maximal ideal is provided
as a definition to be invoked by hand rather than found automatically, so that
in specific cases a computable inverse can be used instead. The integers modulo
$n$ are defined independently of quotients and identified with
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ by an explicit isomorphism, while the ring obtained by
adjoining a root of a polynomial is defined directly as a quotient.
