The **free group** on a set $S$ is the [group](trough:Group) whose elements are
the words in the letters of $S$ and their formal inverses, with no relations
imposed beyond those that every group must satisfy. It is the most general
group generated by a copy of $S$: every map from $S$ into a group extends to a
homomorphism in exactly one way. Because of this, every group is a quotient of
a free group, and describing a group by generators and relations amounts to
describing such a quotient.

## Definition

Fix a set $S$ and introduce, for each $s \in S$, a new symbol $s^{-1}$. A word
is a finite sequence of letters, each of the form $s$ or $s^{-1}$. An
elementary reduction deletes two adjacent letters of the form $s\,s^{-1}$ or
$s^{-1}s$, and two words are called equivalent when one can be turned into the
other by a finite chain of reductions and their reverses. The
free group $F(S)$ is the set of equivalence classes, with
multiplication given by concatenating words. The empty word is the identity,
and the inverse of a word is obtained by reversing it and inverting each
letter. Each $s \in S$ gives the element of $F(S)$ represented by the one-letter
word $s$, and distinct elements of $S$ give distinct elements of
$F(S)$.

A word is reduced if no elementary reduction applies to it. Reduction is
confluent: if a word reduces to two words, both of these reduce to a common
word. It follows that every equivalence
class contains exactly one reduced word, so an element of $F(S)$ can be named by
its reduced word: the word obtained by reducing as far as possible
is reduced, and
distinct elements have distinct reduced
words. For instance, with $S = \{a, b\}$, the
word $a\,b\,b^{-1}a^{-1}b$ reduces to $b$.

## Universal property

Let $G$ be any group and $f \colon S \to G$ any map. Then there is a unique
[homomorphism](trough:MonoidHom) $\varphi \colon F(S) \to G$ that sends each
generator $s$ to $f(s)$; it sends a word $s_1^{\varepsilon_1} \cdots
s_n^{\varepsilon_n}$, with each $\varepsilon_i = \pm 1$, to the product
$f(s_1)^{\varepsilon_1} \cdots f(s_n)^{\varepsilon_n}$. In other words,
restricting to the generators is a bijection between homomorphisms
$F(S) \to G$ and maps $S \to G$. In particular,
two homomorphisms out of $F(S)$ that agree on the generators are
equal. This property determines $F(S)$ up to
isomorphism. A group $G$ is called free when it is isomorphic to $F(S)$ for some
set $S$; the image of $S$ in $G$ under such an isomorphism is then called a
basis of $G$.

## Examples

- If $S$ is empty, $F(S)$ is the trivial group.
- If $S$ has one element, $F(S)$ is infinite cyclic: it is isomorphic to the
  additive group of the integers, the
  element $s^n$ corresponding to $n$ (see [integer](trough:Int) and
  [cyclic group](trough:IsCyclic)).
- If $S = \{a, b\}$, the free group $F_2$ on two generators is not abelian;
  indeed, two generators that commute must be
  equal. More generally,
  $F(S)$ is commutative exactly when $S$ has at most one
  element.
- In topology, the fundamental group of a wedge of $n$ circles is the free
  group on $n$ generators.

## Properties

- **Every group is a quotient of a free group.** For a group $G$, the
  homomorphism from the free group on the underlying set of $G$ that sends each
  generator to itself is surjective. More
  precisely, for any map $f \colon S \to G$ the
  image of the induced homomorphism is the subgroup generated by
  $f(S)$ (see
  [subgroup generated by a subset](trough:Subgroup.closure)). By the
  [first isomorphism theorem](trough:QuotientGroup.quotientKerEquivRange),
  $G$ is then isomorphic to a quotient of a free group by a
  [normal subgroup](trough:Subgroup.Normal). Choosing generators, together with a set of
  relators whose normal closure is this kernel, gives a
  [presentation](trough:PresentedGroup) of $G$.
- **Rank.** The cardinality of a basis is an invariant: if two free groups are
  isomorphic, their bases are in bijection. One
  way to see this passes to the [abelianization](trough:Group.Abelianization) of
  $F(S)$, which is the free abelian group on $S$, and uses the invariance of
  the rank of a free abelian group. The common cardinality is the rank of the
  free group.
- **Subgroups are free.** The [Nielsen–Schreier
  theorem](trough:Subgroup.isFreeGroupOfIsFreeGroup) states that
  every subgroup of a free group is
  free. The subgroup can have larger
  rank than the group: a subgroup of index $k$ in a free group of finite rank
  $n$ is free of rank $k(n - 1) + 1$, and the commutator subgroup of $F_2$ is
  free of infinite rank.
- **Torsion.** A free group has no elements of finite order other than the
  identity. In fact more is true: if $x^n = y^n$ for some positive integer $n$, then
  $x = y$, so $n$-th roots are unique when they exist.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines the free group on an arbitrary type, which may be infinite.
A word is a list of pairs, each consisting of a generator and a flag that
records whether the letter is the generator or its inverse, and the free group
is the quotient of the type of such lists by the equivalence relation generated
by a single elementary reduction. Computing the reduced word of an element
assumes that equality of generators is decidable, which is always available
classically. The property of being free is recorded separately:
a group is free when it admits a free basis, meaning an
isomorphism with the free group on some type, and the Nielsen–Schreier theorem
is stated for this property. Torsion-freeness is obtained as a consequence of
the stronger fact that free groups have
unique roots, meaning that taking $n$-th powers is injective for every
positive integer $n$, which Mathlib proves for every free group
through an unnamed instance. An additive copy of the whole theory, the free
additive group, is generated automatically; despite its name it is not
commutative once there are two generators.
