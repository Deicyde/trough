A **chain complex** is a sequence of objects, such as abelian groups or [modules](trough:Module) over a [ring](trough:Ring), joined by maps that lower the degree by one, in which any two consecutive maps compose to zero. It is the basic object of homological algebra. The vanishing of these composites says that the image of each map lies inside the kernel of the next, and the failure of the two to coincide is measured by the [homology](trough:HomologicalComplex.homology) of the complex. Chain complexes arise from topological spaces, from resolutions of modules and from many other structures, and homology turns them into invariants that can be computed.

## Definition

A chain complex $X$ of $R$-modules, indexed by the [integers](trough:Int), consists of a module $X_n$ for each integer $n$ and [linear maps](trough:LinearMap) $d_n \colon X_n \to X_{n-1}$, the *differentials* or *boundary maps*, such that

$$d_{n-1} \circ d_n = 0 \quad \text{for every } n.$$

It is usually drawn as
$$\cdots \to X_{n+1} \xrightarrow{d_{n+1}} X_n \xrightarrow{d_n} X_{n-1} \to \cdots$$
The same definition makes sense with the modules replaced by objects of any [category](trough:CategoryTheory.Category) that has zero morphisms, and with other index sets, the most common being the [natural numbers](trough:Nat), where the complex stops at $X_0$. Mathlib defines [a chain complex](lean:ChainComplex) in this generality, and in it [the composite of two consecutive differentials is zero](lean:HomologicalComplex.d_comp_d). Over the integers or the natural numbers, [any family of objects $X_n$ with maps $d_{n+1} \colon X_{n+1} \to X_n$ satisfying $d_{n+1} \circ d_{n+2} = 0$ for every $n$ assembles into a chain complex](lean:ChainComplex.of).

Elements of $X_n$ are called $n$-chains. The [kernel](trough:LinearMap.ker) $Z_n = \ker d_n$ consists of the $n$-cycles and the [image](trough:LinearMap.range) $B_n = \operatorname{im} d_{n+1}$ of the $n$-boundaries. The condition $d_n \circ d_{n+1} = 0$ says exactly that $B_n \subseteq Z_n$, and the $n$-th homology is the quotient
$$H_n(X) = Z_n / B_n.$$
The complex is *exact* at $n$ when $B_n = Z_n$, that is, when $H_n(X) = 0$. In Mathlib's general setting, for any complex whose homology in degree $n$ is defined, [the complex is exact at $n$ if and only if its homology in degree $n$ is zero](lean:HomologicalComplex.exactAt_iff_isZero_homology).

A *chain map* $f \colon X \to Y$ is a family of maps $f_n \colon X_n \to Y_n$ with $d^Y_n \circ f_n = f_{n-1} \circ d^X_n$ for all $n$. Mathlib defines [chain maps](lean:HomologicalComplex.Hom) and records that [each one commutes with the differentials](lean:HomologicalComplex.Hom.comm). With chain maps as morphisms, chain complexes form a category, and when the objects lie in an [abelian category](trough:CategoryTheory.Abelian), such as the [category of modules over a ring](trough:ModuleCat), the chain complexes form an abelian category again, with kernels and cokernels computed degree by degree.

## Examples

- **A periodic exact complex.** Let every $X_n$ be $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and every $d_n$ multiplication by $2$. Since $2 \cdot 2 = 4 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, this is a chain complex. Both the kernel and the image of multiplication by $2$ are $\{0, 2\}$, so the complex is exact everywhere, although no term is zero.
- **A two-term complex.** The complex with $X_1 = X_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $d_1$ multiplication by $m \geq 1$, and all other terms zero has $H_1 = 0$ and $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Simplicial chains.** Take the boundary of a triangle, with vertices $a, b, c$ and edges $[a,b]$, $[b,c]$, $[c,a]$. Let $X_1$ be the free abelian group on the edges, $X_0$ the free abelian group on the vertices, and $d_1[x,y] = y - x$, with all other terms zero. The image of $d_1$ is the set of combinations of vertices whose coefficients sum to zero, so $H_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}$, while $H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the cycle $[a,b] + [b,c] + [c,a]$. This detects the single loop of the triangle's boundary, which is a circle.
- **Singular chains.** For a [topological space](trough:TopologicalSpace) $T$, the free abelian groups on continuous maps from the standard $n$-simplex into $T$, with the alternating sum of the faces as differential, form [the singular chain complex](lean:AlgebraicTopology.singularChainComplexFunctor), indexed by the natural numbers. Its homology is the singular homology of $T$. Mathlib builds it with coefficients in any additive category with coproducts; coefficients in the integers give the complex just described.
- **Resolutions.** A projective resolution of a module $M$ is a chain complex of projective modules $\cdots \to P_1 \to P_0$, indexed by the natural numbers, together with a map $P_0 \to M$ that makes $\cdots \to P_1 \to P_0 \to M \to 0$ exact; equivalently, the induced map $H_0(P) \to M$ is an isomorphism and the higher homology vanishes. Mathlib defines [projective resolutions](lean:CategoryTheory.ProjectiveResolution) for objects of any category with a zero object and zero morphisms, as a complex of projective objects with a quasi-isomorphism (see below) to the complex that has $M$ in degree $0$ and zero elsewhere. Resolutions are the input to derived functors such as Tor and Ext.

## Properties

- **Functoriality of homology.** A chain map $f$ sends cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, so it [induces maps $H_n(X) \to H_n(Y)$](lean:HomologicalComplex.homologyMap), and [the map induced by a composite is the composite of the induced maps](lean:HomologicalComplex.homologyMap_comp).
- **Homotopy invariance.** Two chain maps $f, g \colon X \to Y$ between complexes in an additive setting, such as modules, are *chain homotopic* if there are maps $h_n \colon X_n \to Y_{n+1}$ with
$$f_n - g_n = d^Y_{n+1} \circ h_n + h_{n-1} \circ d^X_n$$
for all $n$; Mathlib calls such a family [a homotopy](lean:Homotopy). [Homotopic chain maps induce the same maps on homology](lean:Homotopy.homologyMap_eq).
- **Quasi-isomorphisms.** For complexes in an abelian category, such as complexes of modules, a chain map is a [quasi-isomorphism](lean:QuasiIso) when it induces isomorphisms on all homology objects. [Every homotopy equivalence is a quasi-isomorphism](lean:HomotopyEquiv.quasiIso_hom), but the converse fails in general.
- **The long exact sequence.** In an abelian category, a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ of chain complexes yields a [connecting morphism](lean:CategoryTheory.ShortComplex.ShortExact.δ) $\delta \colon H_n(C) \to H_{n-1}(A)$ for each $n$, and the resulting sequence
$$\cdots \to H_n(A) \to H_n(B) \to H_n(C) \xrightarrow{\delta} H_{n-1}(A) \to H_{n-1}(B) \to \cdots$$
is exact: [at $H_n(B)$](lean:CategoryTheory.ShortComplex.ShortExact.homology_exact₂), [at $H_n(C)$](lean:CategoryTheory.ShortComplex.ShortExact.homology_exact₃) and [at $H_{n-1}(A)$](lean:CategoryTheory.ShortComplex.ShortExact.homology_exact₁).

## Related concepts

A *cochain complex* has differentials that raise the degree, $d^n \colon X^n \to X^{n+1}$; Mathlib defines [cochain complexes](lean:CochainComplex) alongside chain complexes. Over the integers the two notions are interchangeable by setting $X^n = X_{-n}$; homology theories are usually written with chain complexes, cohomology theories with cochain complexes. Double complexes, which carry two compatible differentials, and the derived category, in which quasi-isomorphisms are inverted, build further on chain complexes.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib treats chain complexes as one case of a single notion, [a homological complex](lean:HomologicalComplex), whose shape records which pairs of indices may carry a nonzero differential. A chain complex is a homological complex of the shape in which the differential from index $i$ to index $j$ is allowed only when $i = j + 1$; a cochain complex is the shape with $j = i + 1$. The index set may be any type with an addition, cancellative on the right, and an element $1$, which covers the integers and the natural numbers. The terms may lie in any category with zero morphisms. A complex carries a map $X_i \to X_j$ for every pair of indices, required to be zero outside the allowed pairs; this avoids having to identify $X_{j+1}$ with $X_i$ whenever $i = j + 1$ holds as a proof rather than as a definition. Homology in a given degree is defined when the complex has suitable homology data there, compatible kernel and cokernel constructions; this is automatic in an abelian category such as modules over a ring.
