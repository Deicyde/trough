The **Hilbert basis theorem** says that if $R$ is a commutative [Noetherian ring](trough:IsNoetherianRing), then so is the [polynomial ring](trough:Polynomial) $R[x]$. Applied repeatedly, it shows that every [ideal](trough:Ideal) of a polynomial ring in finitely many variables over a field, or over the integers, is generated by finitely many polynomials. It is one of the basic finiteness results of commutative algebra and algebraic geometry: it guarantees that any system of polynomial equations, however large, has the same solutions as some finite subsystem.

## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative Noetherian ring, so that every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. Then
$R[x]$ is Noetherian as well: for each ideal $I$ of $R[x]$ there are polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_m \in I$ such that every element of $I$ has the form

$$g_1 f_1 + \cdots + g_m f_m, \qquad g_1, \dots, g_m \in R[x].$$

By induction on the number of variables,
the polynomial ring $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ in finitely many variables is Noetherian (see [polynomial in several indeterminates](trough:MvPolynomial)), since $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is the polynomial ring in one variable $x_n$ over $R[x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}]$.

## Examples

- Taking $R$ to be a [field](trough:Field) $k$, every ideal of $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is finitely generated. For $n = 1$ this is already clear, because $k[x]$ is a [principal ideal domain](trough:Submodule.IsPrincipal); for $n \ge 2$ the ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is not a principal ideal domain (the ideal $(x_1, x_2)$ is not principal), and the theorem gives finite generation, though no bound on the number of generators.
- Taking $R$ to be the [integers](trough:Int), the rings $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[x, y]$ are Noetherian. Neither is a principal ideal domain: in both rings the ideal $(2, x)$ is not principal.
- The ring of Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$, and
  a quotient of a commutative Noetherian ring by an ideal is Noetherian (see [quotient ring](trough:Ideal.Quotient.ring)), so $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Noetherian.
- The hypothesis of finitely many variables cannot be dropped: in $k[x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots]$ the chain $(x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2, x_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ never stops, so the ideal generated by all the variables is not finitely generated.

## Consequences

- **Finitely generated algebras.** Every finitely generated commutative algebra over $R$ is a quotient of a polynomial ring $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. Combining the theorem with the statement about quotients,
  every finitely generated commutative algebra over a commutative Noetherian ring is Noetherian (see [associative algebra](trough:Algebra)).
- **Algebraic sets.** If $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is any set of polynomials, the ideal it generates has finitely many generators, each a combination of finitely many elements of $S$. So the common zero set of $S$ in $k^n$ is the common zero set of finitely many members of $S$. Together with the [Nullstellensatz](trough:MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal_zeroLocus_eq_radical), this is the starting point of classical algebraic geometry.
- **The converse.** The ring $R$ is the quotient of $R[x]$ by the ideal $(x)$, and
  the image of a Noetherian ring under a surjective ring homomorphism is Noetherian. Hence $R[x]$ is Noetherian if and only if $R$ is.
- **Power series.** The same conclusion holds for formal power series:
  the ring of formal power series in finitely many variables over a commutative Noetherian ring is Noetherian (see [power series](trough:PowerSeries)).
- **Dimension.** The Krull dimension of a ring is the supremum of the lengths of chains of prime ideals in it. For a commutative Noetherian ring $R$,
  the Krull dimension of $R[x]$ is one more than that of $R$, with the conventions that the zero ring has dimension $-\infty$ and that $\infty + 1 = \infty$.

## Proof

Let $I$ be an ideal of $R[x]$. For each $n \ge 0$ let $L_n$ be the set of leading coefficients of the polynomials in $I$ of degree at most $n$, the zero polynomial contributing $0$. Equivalently,
$L_n$ is the set of coefficients of $x^n$ in the polynomials of $I$ of degree at most $n$, a description under which it is visibly closed under addition and under multiplication by elements of $R$; so $L_n$ is an ideal of $R$. Moreover multiplying a polynomial by a power of $x$ does not change its leading coefficient, so
$L_m \subseteq L_n$ whenever $m \le n$. Because $R$ is Noetherian, this increasing chain stops: there is an $N$ with $L_n = L_N$ for all $n \ge N$.

The polynomials of degree at most $N$ form a finitely generated $R$-module, so the submodule of those lying in $I$ is finitely generated too, $R$ being Noetherian:
$I$ contains finitely many polynomials of degree at most $N$ that span, over $R$, all the polynomials in $I$ of degree at most $N$. Let $J$ be the ideal of $R[x]$ that they generate; then $J \subseteq I$.

Every $p \in I$ lies in $J$. The case $p = 0$ is clear; otherwise argue by induction on the degree $k$ of $p$. If $k \le N$, this holds by the choice of the generators. If $k > N$, the leading coefficient of $p$ lies in $L_k = L_N$, so it is the leading coefficient of some $q \in I$ of degree $d \le N$, and $q \in J$. The polynomial $p - x^{k-d} q$ lies in $I$ and is either zero or of degree less than $k$, so it lies in $J$ by induction, and therefore so does $p$. Hence $I = J$ is finitely generated.

## Generalizations

The theorem holds without commutativity: if every left ideal of a ring $R$ is finitely generated, the same is true of $R[x]$, by the same argument. The several-variable form above holds for any finite set of variables, and the analogue for power series is listed among the consequences.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib states the theorem for commutative rings. A ring is Noetherian in Mathlib when it is a [Noetherian module](trough:IsNoetherian) over itself, which amounts to every ideal being finitely generated. The theorem is registered so that the Noetherian property of a polynomial ring is found automatically from that of its coefficient ring. The several-variable version allows the variables to be indexed by any finite type rather than by the numbers $1, \dots, n$. Instead of an increasing chain of ideals that stabilizes, the formal proof uses an equivalent form of the Noetherian condition: among the ideals $L_n$ it picks one that is maximal, which then contains all the others.
