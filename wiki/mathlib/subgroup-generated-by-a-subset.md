The **subgroup generated by a subset** $S$ of a [group](trough:Group) $G$ is the
smallest [subgroup](trough:Subgroup) of $G$ that contains $S$. It is written
$\langle S \rangle$, and its elements are exactly the finite products of elements
of $S$ and their inverses. Groups are often described by a few generators: a
rotation and a reflection generate a dihedral group, for example, and many
questions about the whole group, such as whether two homomorphisms out of it
agree, reduce to questions about the generators.

## Definition

Let $G$ be a group and $S \subseteq G$ any subset. The intersection of a family of
subgroups is again a subgroup, so the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ that
contain $S$ is a subgroup. It is
the subgroup generated by $S$:

$$\langle S \rangle = \bigcap \{\, H \le G : S \subseteq H \,\}.$$

By construction $S$ is contained in $\langle S \rangle$,
and $\langle S \rangle$ is the least subgroup with this property:
for a subgroup $K$, $\langle S \rangle \le K$ holds exactly when $S \subseteq K$.
If $\langle S \rangle = G$, one says that $S$ generates $G$, or that $S$ is a set
of generators of $G$.

The intersection describes $\langle S \rangle$ from above. From below,
$\langle S \rangle$ consists of the identity and all products

$$s_1^{\varepsilon_1} s_2^{\varepsilon_2} \cdots s_n^{\varepsilon_n}, \qquad s_i \in S,\ \varepsilon_i \in \{1, -1\}.$$

These products contain $S$ and form a subgroup, since a product of two of them
is another, and so is the inverse of one; every subgroup containing $S$ contains
all of them. Equivalently, writing $S^{-1} = \{s^{-1} : s \in S\}$,
$\langle S \rangle$ coincides with the submonoid generated by $S \cup S^{-1}$,
the smallest subset containing $S \cup S^{-1}$ and the identity and closed under
products. In any monoid,
the submonoid generated by a set consists of the finite products of elements of that set,
the identity being the empty product; so the elements of $\langle S \rangle$ are
the finite products of elements of $S \cup S^{-1}$.
Minimality gives an induction principle: to show that a property holds on
all of $\langle S \rangle$, it suffices to check it on the elements of $S$ and the
identity, and to show it is preserved by products and inverses.

## Examples

- **The empty set.** The empty set generates the trivial subgroup
  $\{e\}$, the empty product being the identity $e$.
- **One element.** The subgroup generated by a single element $g$ consists of the
  integer powers $g^n$. A group generated by
  one element is a [cyclic group](trough:IsCyclic), and the order of that subgroup is
  the [order](trough:orderOf) of $g$ (both are infinite when $g$ has infinite
  order).
- **Integers.** In the additive group $\mathbb{Z}$, the subgroup generated by
  integers $a$ and $b$ is the set of combinations $ma + nb$ with $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$, which equals
  $d\mathbb{Z}$ for $d = \gcd(a, b)$. Thus $\{2, 3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$, while
  $\{4, 6\}$ generates $2\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Dihedral groups.** The symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon is generated by a
  rotation $r$ through $2\pi/n$ together with any one reflection $s$; every symmetry is
  $r^k$ or $r^k s$.
- **Symmetric groups.** In the [permutation group](trough:Equiv.Perm) of a finite set,
  the transpositions generate the whole group.

## Properties

- **Monotonicity.** If $S \subseteq T$, then $\langle S \rangle \le \langle T \rangle$.
- **Subgroups are their own closure.** For a subgroup $H$, $\langle H \rangle = H$.
- **Joins.** The subgroup generated by a union is the smallest subgroup containing
  both pieces: $\langle S \cup T \rangle = \langle S \rangle \vee \langle T \rangle$.
  In particular the join of two subgroups, which is larger than their union
  unless one subgroup contains the other, is the subgroup generated by the union.
- **Images.** For a [group homomorphism](trough:MonoidHom) $f \colon G \to G'$,
  the image of $\langle S \rangle$ is the subgroup generated by $f(S)$.
  By the induction principle, two homomorphisms that agree on a generating set
  are equal.
- **Finite generation.** A group is finitely generated when some finite subset
  generates it; Mathlib's notion of finite generation is equivalent to this.
  Every finite group is finitely generated, while the additive group $\mathbb{Q}$
  is not.

## Related concepts

The subgroup generated by $S$ need not be normal. The smallest
[normal subgroup](trough:Subgroup.Normal) containing $S$, the normal closure, is
the subgroup generated by all conjugates of elements of $S$;
normal closures also define a [presented group](trough:PresentedGroup), the quotient of
a [free group](trough:FreeGroup) by the normal closure of its set of relators. The products of generators
and their inverses are modelled abstractly by the free group on $S$:
for every group $G$ and map $S \to G$ whose image generates $G$, the induced
homomorphism from the free group on $S$ to $G$ is surjective.
[Schreier's lemma](trough:Subgroup.closure_mul_image_eq) produces generators for a
subgroup from generators of the whole group and a choice of coset representatives.
The same pattern, the least substructure containing a given set, defines the span
of a set of vectors in a vector space; see [generating sets](trough:Submodule.span).

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines the subgroup generated by a set as the infimum of all subgroups
containing it in the complete lattice of subgroups, which is the intersection
above; the description by products is a theorem rather than the definition. The
subgroup is a bundled structure carrying its set of elements and the proofs of the
subgroup axioms. The facts that the closure contains the set, is least among
subgroups containing it, and returns every subgroup unchanged
($\langle H \rangle = H$) are packaged as
a Galois insertion between subsets and subgroups, from which
monotonicity and the formulas for unions follow. An additive version is generated
automatically for additive groups, so the same results hold for subgroups of
$\mathbb{Z}$ or of any group written additively. Finite generation is
defined through generation as a semigroup by a finite set, which for a group
agrees with the textbook notion.
