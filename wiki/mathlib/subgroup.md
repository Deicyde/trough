A **subgroup** of a [group](trough:Group) $G$ is a subset of $G$ that is itself a group under
the same operation. Subgroups are the basic way of locating smaller groups inside a
larger one: much of the structure theory of groups, from [Lagrange's
theorem](trough:Subgroup.card_subgroup_dvd_card) to the Sylow theorems, is a study of
which subgroups a group has and how they sit inside it.

## Definition

Let $G$ be a group with identity element $e$. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a
[subgroup](lean:Subgroup) if

- $e \in H$;
- $xy \in H$ whenever $x, y \in H$;
- $x^{-1} \in H$ whenever $x \in H$.

One writes $H \le G$. The three conditions say exactly that the operation of $G$
restricts to $H$ and that the restricted operation satisfies the group axioms, so
[a subgroup is a group in its own right](lean:Subgroup.toGroup), and the inclusion of
$H$ into $G$ is [a group homomorphism](lean:Subgroup.subtype). Associativity needs
no check, since it is inherited from $G$.

The conditions can be condensed into a single test: [a nonempty subset $H$ such that
$xy^{-1} \in H$ for all $x, y \in H$ is a subgroup](lean:Subgroup.ofDiv). Indeed,
taking $y = x$ gives $e \in H$, then taking $x = e$ gives closure under inverses, and
closure under products follows by applying the test to $x$ and $y^{-1}$.

## Examples

- Every group $G$ has the **trivial subgroup** $\{e\}$ and the whole group $G$ as
  subgroups. A subgroup other than $G$ is called proper.
- In the additive group of [integers](trough:Int), the multiples of a fixed integer
  $n$ form a subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$, and [an integer $b$ lies in it exactly when $n$
  divides $b$](lean:Int.mem_zmultiples_iff). Every subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ has this
  form.
- For an element $g \in G$, the powers $g^k$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ form [the cyclic
  subgroup generated by $g$](lean:Subgroup.zpowers), which is [the smallest subgroup
  containing $g$](lean:Subgroup.zpowers_le); its size is the [order](trough:orderOf) of $g$ when that order is finite.
- The [center](lean:Subgroup.center) of $G$, the set of elements that commute with
  every element of $G$, is a subgroup.
- For a homomorphism $f \colon G \to K$ of groups, the [kernel](lean:MonoidHom.ker),
  the set of $g$ with $f(g) = e$, is a subgroup of $G$, and the
  [image](lean:MonoidHom.range) is a subgroup of $K$.
- The [alternating group](trough:alternatingGroup) $A_n$ of even permutations is a
  subgroup of the symmetric group on $n$ letters.

## Properties

- **Intersections.** [The intersection of any family of subgroups is a
  subgroup](lean:Subgroup.coe_sInf). This makes it possible to speak of the smallest
  subgroup containing a given subset, the [subgroup generated by
  it](trough:Subgroup.closure).
- **Unions.** A union of two subgroups is usually not a subgroup: in $\mathbb{Z}$, the
  set $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 3\mathbb{Z}$ contains $2$ and $3$ but not $5$. More precisely,
  [a subgroup contained in the union of two subgroups is contained in one of
  them](lean:SubgroupClass.subset_union); so the union of two subgroups is a subgroup
  only when one of them contains the other.
- **The lattice of subgroups.** Ordered by inclusion, the subgroups of $G$ form a
  complete lattice: the greatest lower bound of a family is its intersection, and the
  least upper bound is the subgroup generated by its union. The trivial subgroup is the
  least element and $G$ the greatest.
- **Order.** If $G$ is finite, [the order of every subgroup divides the order of
  $G$](lean:Subgroup.card_subgroup_dvd_card). In particular, [a group of prime order
  has no subgroups other than the trivial one and itself](lean:Subgroup.eq_bot_or_eq_top_of_prime_card).
- **Cyclic groups.** [Every subgroup of a cyclic group is
  cyclic](lean:Subgroup.isCyclic) (see [cyclic group](trough:IsCyclic)); this is why
  each subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form $n\mathbb{Z}$.

## Related concepts

The cosets $gH$ of a subgroup $H$ partition $G$, and their number is the
[index](trough:Subgroup.index) of $H$ in $G$. A subgroup that is invariant under
conjugation, so that $gHg^{-1} = H$ for every $g$, is a [normal
subgroup](trough:Subgroup.Normal); normal subgroups are exactly the kernels of
homomorphisms, and the cosets of a normal subgroup form the [quotient
group](trough:QuotientGroup.Quotient.group). A nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups
are the trivial subgroup and the whole group is a [simple group](trough:IsSimpleGroup).
[Cayley's theorem](trough:Equiv.Perm.subgroupOfMulAction) shows that every group $G$ is
isomorphic to a subgroup of the [group of permutations](trough:Equiv.Perm) of its
underlying set. The same idea of
a subset closed under the operations of a structure gives the
[subring](trough:Subring), the [subfield](trough:Subfield) and the [vector
subspace](trough:Subspace).

## In Mathlib

Mathlib treats a subgroup as a bundled object: a subset of the group packaged together
with proofs of the three closure properties, built by extending the notion of a
submonoid with closure under inverses. Two subgroups are equal as soon as [they have
the same elements](lean:Subgroup.ext). A subgroup is not a separate type, but it can
be used as one, and it then carries the group structure inherited from the ambient
group. Subgroups are ordered by inclusion and form a complete lattice, with the
trivial subgroup as least element and the whole group as greatest. The kernel is
defined more generally for a homomorphism from a group into any type with a
multiplication and an identity element, in particular any monoid. The additive
version, for groups written with $+$, is generated automatically from the
multiplicative one; the statements about $\mathbb{Z}$ above are phrased in terms of it. A
separate class covers any kind of object that behaves like a subgroup, so that, for
example, a subring can be treated directly as an additive subgroup of its ring, and some general results, like the one on
unions, are stated at that level.
