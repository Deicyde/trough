A **Noetherian ring** is a [ring](trough:Ring) in which every [ideal](trough:Ideal) is finitely generated; equivalently, every increasing chain of ideals eventually stops growing. The condition is a finiteness hypothesis that covers most rings met in number theory and algebraic geometry, including the integers, fields, and polynomial rings in finitely many variables over them. It is preserved by the usual constructions of commutative algebra, and it is the standing assumption under which much of the subject's structure theory holds: primary decomposition, finiteness of minimal primes, and the dimension theory of rings.

## Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative ring. The following conditions on $R$ are equivalent.

1. Every ideal $I$ of $R$ is finitely generated: there are $a_1, \dots, a_n \in I$ with $I = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$.
2. (Ascending chain condition.) For every chain of ideals $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ there is an $N$ with $I_n = I_N$ for all $n \ge N$.
3. (Maximal condition.) Every nonempty set of ideals of $R$ has a maximal element with respect to inclusion.

The ring $R$ is Noetherian if it satisfies them. In Mathlib's terms, $R$ is Noetherian when it is a [Noetherian module](trough:IsNoetherian) over itself, and this holds if and only if every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. Mathlib proves the other two characterizations for an arbitrary module: a module is Noetherian if and only if every increasing chain of submodules stabilizes, and if and only if every nonempty set of submodules has a maximal member. Taking the module to be $R$ itself, whose submodules are its ideals, gives conditions 2 and 3.

To see that condition 1 gives condition 2, note that the union $I$ of a chain of ideals is again an ideal. Its finitely many generators all lie in some $I_N$, so $I = I_N$ and the chain is constant from $N$ on. Conversely, if an ideal $I$ were not finitely generated, one could choose $a_1, a_2, \dots \in I$ with each $a_{n+1}$ outside $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$, producing a chain that never stops. Condition 3 gives condition 2 by applying it to the set of ideals $\{I_n\}$ in a chain: a maximal member $I_N$ forces $I_n = I_N$ for $n \ge N$. Conversely, a nonempty set of ideals with no maximal member contains a strictly increasing infinite chain, so condition 2 gives condition 3.

## Examples

- A [field](trough:Field) has only two ideals, $0$ and the whole field, so it is Noetherian.
- In a [principal ideal domain](trough:Submodule.IsPrincipal) every ideal is generated by one element, and indeed every principal ideal ring is Noetherian. This covers the [integers](trough:Int) and the polynomial ring $k[x]$ over a field $k$.
- Every finite ring, such as $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \ge 1$, is Noetherian, as is more generally every module with finitely many elements.
- **[Hilbert basis theorem](trough:Polynomial.isNoetherianRing).** If $R$ is a commutative Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring $R[x]$ is Noetherian. By induction, polynomial rings in finitely many variables over $R$ are Noetherian (see [polynomial in several indeterminates](trough:MvPolynomial)). A variant of the argument shows that the ring $R[[x]]$ of formal power series is Noetherian, and by the same induction so is the ring of formal power series in finitely many variables over $R$ (see [power series](trough:PowerSeries)).
- If $R$ is a commutative Noetherian ring and $I$ an ideal, then the quotient ring $R/I$ is Noetherian (see [quotient ring](trough:Ideal.Quotient.ring)), and every localization of $R$ is Noetherian (see [localization](trough:Localization)). Since a finitely generated commutative $R$-algebra is a quotient of a polynomial ring in finitely many variables over $R$, every finitely generated commutative algebra over a commutative Noetherian ring is Noetherian.

The polynomial ring $k[x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables is not Noetherian, since $(x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2, x_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ never stops. Because this ring is a subring of its [field of fractions](trough:IsFractionRing), which is Noetherian, the property does not pass to subrings.

## Properties

Let $R$ be a commutative Noetherian ring.

- **Modules.** Every finitely generated $R$-module is a Noetherian module: all of its submodules are finitely generated. In a Noetherian module, a surjective linear map from the module to itself is injective.
- **Factorization.** If $R$ is an integral domain, strict divisibility in $R$ admits no infinite descending chains, and therefore every nonzero element is, up to a unit, a product of irreducible elements (see [irreducible element](trough:Irreducible)). The factorization need not be unique: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is Noetherian but not a [unique factorization domain](trough:UniqueFactorizationMonoid), since $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$.
- **Primary decomposition.** By the [Lasker–Noether theorem](trough:Submodule.isPrimary_decomposition_pairwise_ne_radical), every ideal of $R$, and more generally every submodule of a Noetherian module over a commutative ring, is a finite intersection of primary ones, the whole ring or module counting as the empty intersection.
- **Minimal primes and nilradical.** $R$ has only finitely many minimal prime ideals (see [prime ideal](trough:Ideal.IsPrime)), and its nilradical, the ideal of nilpotent elements, is nilpotent: some power of it is the zero ideal.
- **Krull's intersection theorem.** For a proper ideal $I$ of $R$,
  $$\bigcap_{n \ge 1} I^n = 0$$
  holds when $R$ is an integral domain and when $R$ is a local ring (see [local ring](trough:IsLocalRing)).
- **Dimension.** By [Krull's principal ideal theorem](trough:Ideal.height_le_one_of_isPrincipal_of_mem_minimalPrimes), a prime ideal of $R$ that is minimal among primes containing a principal ideal has height at most $1$. A commutative ring is Artinian, meaning that descending chains of ideals stop, if and only if it is Noetherian and every prime ideal is maximal (see [maximal ideal](trough:Ideal.IsMaximal)).

## Generalizations

The definition makes sense for a noncommutative ring, where the left and right versions differ: a ring is left Noetherian if its left ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition, and right Noetherian if its right ideals do. The module-theoretic version, a [Noetherian module](trough:IsNoetherian), is the condition that every submodule is finitely generated, and a ring is Noetherian exactly when it is a Noetherian module over itself. The dual condition on descending chains defines Artinian rings and modules.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines the notion for any semiring, not only commutative rings, and makes it an abbreviation: a semiring is Noetherian when it is a Noetherian module over itself, a module being Noetherian when every submodule is finitely generated. Since Mathlib's ideals of a noncommutative ring are its left ideals, for such rings the definition means left Noetherian. The chain conditions are expressed through well-foundedness of strict inclusion, read in reverse, on the lattice of submodules. Being Noetherian is a class, so the property of polynomial rings in finitely many variables, quotients, the standard localization at a submonoid, and principal ideal rings is supplied automatically wherever it is needed. The Hilbert basis theorem and most of the structure theory are stated for commutative rings.
