# Nilpotent group

A **nilpotent group** is a [group](group.md) that can be built from abelian
pieces by repeatedly taking central extensions (extensions in which the
kernel lies in the center), in finitely many steps. Every abelian group is
nilpotent, and so is every finite $p$-group. Nilpotency sits
between commutativity and solvability: nilpotent groups keep much of the
arithmetic control that abelian groups enjoy, which makes them a basic class in
finite group theory, in the theory of discrete groups and in Lie theory.

## Definition

Write $Z(G)$ for the center of $G$ and $[x, y] = xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ for the
commutator of two elements. The
[*upper central series*](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.upperCentralSeries) of $G$ is the increasing
chain of [normal subgroups](normal-subgroup.md)

$$1 = Z_0 \le Z_1 \le Z_2 \le \cdots$$

in which $Z_{i+1}$ consists of the elements $x$ with $[x, y] \in Z_i$ for every
$y \in G$; equivalently, $Z_{i+1}/Z_i$ is the center of the
[quotient group](quotient-group.md) $G/Z_i$. Thus $Z_1 = Z(G)$.
The group $G$ is
[nilpotent if $Z_c = G$ for some $c$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent), and the least such
$c$ is the [nilpotency class](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.nilpotencyClass) of $G$. The trivial
group has class $0$, and a nilpotent group has
[class at most $1$ exactly when it is abelian](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent.nilpotencyClass_le_one_iff),
so a nontrivial group has class $1$ exactly when it is abelian.

There are two equivalent descriptions. The *lower central series* is defined by
$\gamma_1 = G$ and $\gamma_{i+1} = [\gamma_i, G]$, the
[subgroup generated by](subgroup-generated-by-a-subset.md) all commutators $[x, y]$ with
$x \in \gamma_i$ and $y \in G$. A group is
[nilpotent if and only if its lower central series reaches the trivial subgroup](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.nilpotent_iff_lowerCentralSeries),
and $\gamma_{c+1} = 1$
[holds for the same least $c$ as $Z_c = G$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.lowerCentralSeries_length_eq_nilpotencyClass). More flexibly,
$G$ is nilpotent if and only if it has a
[finite central series](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.nilpotent_iff_finite_ascending_central_series),
a finite sequence of subgroups $1 = H_0, H_1, \dots, H_n = G$ with
$[H_{i+1}, G] \le H_i$ for each $i$. Such a sequence need not increase, but if
$G$ is nilpotent then the upper central series, which
[is increasing](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.upperCentralSeries_mono), is one. The upper
series climbs as fast as any such chain and the lower series descends as fast as any such chain, which is why all three descriptions give the
same class.

## Examples

- Abelian groups are [nilpotent](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#CommGroup.isNilpotent), of
  [class at most $1$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#CommGroup.nilpotencyClass_le_one).
- Every [finite $p$-group is nilpotent](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#IsPGroup.isNilpotent), where a finite
  $p$-group is a group whose order is a power of a prime $p$; equivalently, a
  finite group in which every element has order a power of $p$. The proof uses
  the fact that a nontrivial finite $p$-group has nontrivial center and inducts on
  the order. The dihedral group of order $8$ and the quaternion group of order $8$
  are nonabelian examples of class $2$.
- The Heisenberg group of upper triangular $3 \times 3$ integer matrices with ones
  on the diagonal is an infinite nilpotent group of class $2$: its center and its
  commutator subgroup both consist of the matrices whose off-diagonal entries
  are zero except possibly the top right one.
- The [symmetric group](../part-02/permutation-group-of-a-type.md) $S_3$ is not nilpotent. Its center is
  trivial, so its upper central series never leaves $1$. It is nevertheless
  solvable (defined below), so nilpotency is a strictly stronger condition.

## Properties

- **Closure.** [Subgroups](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Subgroup.isNilpotent) and
  [quotients](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.nilpotent_quotient_of_nilpotent) of nilpotent groups are
  nilpotent, and more generally so is
  [the image of a surjective homomorphism](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.nilpotent_of_surjective).
  [Direct products of two nilpotent groups](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.isNilpotent_prod) are
  nilpotent, as are [finite direct products](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.isNilpotent_pi). A group
  with a nilpotent normal subgroup and a nilpotent quotient need not be nilpotent
  ($S_3$ has a normal subgroup of order $3$ with quotient of order $2$), but
  [if $G/Z(G)$ is nilpotent then so is $G$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.of_quotient_center_nilpotent).
- **The center.** A [nontrivial nilpotent group has nontrivial center](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent.center_ne_bot),
  and indeed [every nontrivial normal subgroup meets the center nontrivially](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent.inf_center_ne_bot_of_normal).
- **Solvability.** A group is solvable when its *derived series*
  $G = G^{(0)} \ge G^{(1)} = [G, G] \ge G^{(2)} = [G^{(1)}, G^{(1)}] \ge \cdots$
  eventually reaches $1$. By induction
  [$G^{(i)} \le \gamma_{i+1}$ for every $i$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent/LowerCentralSeries.html#Subgroup.derivedSeries_le_lowerCentralSeries_top),
  so the derived series reaches $1$ no later than the lower central series, and
  [every nilpotent group is solvable](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#IsNilpotent.to_isSolvable).
- **Normalizers.** The normalizer of a subgroup $H$ is the largest subgroup in
  which $H$ is normal. In a nilpotent group
  [every proper subgroup is strictly smaller than its normalizer](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.normalizerCondition_of_isNilpotent).
- **Commuting elements.** In a nilpotent group,
  [two elements whose orders are coprime commute](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent.commute_of_orderOf_coprime).
- **Simple nilpotent groups.** Since the center of a nontrivial nilpotent group is a
  nontrivial normal subgroup, a [simple group](simple-group.md) that is
  nilpotent equals its center; it is therefore abelian, hence
  [cyclic](cyclic-group.md) of prime order by the
  [classification of abelian simple groups](../part-02/classification-of-abelian-simple-groups.md).

## Finite nilpotent groups

For finite groups, nilpotency has several characterizations through the
Sylow $p$-subgroups: for each prime $p$, the subgroups whose order is the
largest power of $p$ dividing $|G|$, whose existence is the
[first Sylow theorem](../part-02/first-sylow-theorem.md). For a
finite group $G$ the following are equivalent: $G$ is nilpotent; every proper
subgroup is strictly smaller than its normalizer; every maximal subgroup (a
proper subgroup contained in no larger proper subgroup) is normal; every
Sylow subgroup is normal; and $G$ is isomorphic to the direct product of its
Sylow subgroups ([the characterization](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.isNilpotent_of_finite_tfae)).
So a finite nilpotent group is nothing more than a direct product of $p$-groups
for distinct primes $p$, and its study reduces to the study of $p$-groups.

One consequence is a full converse to [Lagrange's theorem](../part-02/lagranges-theorem.md)
for this class: if $G$ is finite and nilpotent and $n$ divides $|G|$, then
[$G$ has a normal subgroup of order $n$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsNilpotent.exists_normal_card_eq_of_dvd_card).
The [alternating group](../part-02/alternating-group.md) $A_4$, which has order $12$ but no subgroup of order $6$,
shows that solvable groups do not share this property.

## Related concepts

Nilpotency for [Lie algebras](../../algebras-over-a-ring/lie-algebra.md) is defined the same way, with the
Lie bracket in place of the group commutator; a connected Lie group is nilpotent
exactly when its Lie algebra is. A group is called
[virtually nilpotent](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/GroupTheory/Nilpotent.html#Group.IsVirtuallyNilpotent) if it has a nilpotent
subgroup of finite index. By
Gromov's theorem, a finitely generated group of
polynomial growth is virtually nilpotent; polynomial growth means that the number
of elements expressible as words of length at most $n$ in a fixed finite
generating set is bounded by a polynomial in $n$. Conversely, every finitely generated
virtually nilpotent group has polynomial growth, so polynomial growth
characterizes virtual nilpotency among finitely generated groups.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines nilpotency through the upper central series, a sequence of
subgroups indexed by all natural numbers, and asks that some term equal the whole
group; the lower central series and arbitrary central series appear as
equivalent conditions proved afterwards. Mathlib defines the lower central series of any subgroup $H$ of $G$, computed
inside $G$; the lower central series of $G$ itself is the case $H = G$. It is
indexed from $0$, so its term number $i$ is the textbook $\gamma_{i+1}$.
Ascending and descending central series are infinite sequences required only to
satisfy the commutator condition: they need not be increasing (or decreasing),
need not consist of normal subgroups, and need not reach the top or the bottom.
A $p$-group is defined by the orders of its elements, each a power of $p$, so
finiteness is a separate hypothesis. The nilpotency class is defined for every group, taking the value $0$
when the group is not nilpotent. Nilpotency is a property of a group type, so a
subgroup is nilpotent when it is nilpotent as a group in its own right. Most of
the definitions and general results also have additive versions, stated for
additive groups; the results specific to finite groups do not.
