# Ideal of a commutative ring

An **ideal** of a commutative [ring](../rings/ring.md) $R$ is a nonempty subset of $R$ that
is closed under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary elements of $R$.
Ideals play for rings the part that [normal subgroups](../group-theory/part-01/normal-subgroup.md)
play for groups: they are exactly the kernels of [ring morphisms](../rings/ring-morphism.md),
and they are what one divides by to form [quotient rings](quotient-ring.md).
They also carry the arithmetic of divisibility over from elements to sets, which
is how factorization is studied in rings where elements fail to factor uniquely.

## Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative ring. An [ideal](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Defs.html#Ideal) of $R$ is a subset
$I \subseteq R$ such that

- $0 \in I$;
- if $a, b \in I$, then $a + b \in I$;
- if $b \in I$ and $a \in R$, then [$ab \in I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Defs.html#Ideal.mul_mem_left).

Taking $a = -1$ in the last condition shows that $I$ is closed under negation,
so an ideal is an additive subgroup of $R$ that absorbs multiplication by
elements of $R$. Equivalently, an ideal is a submodule of $R$ regarded as a
[module](../../linear-algebra/fundamentals/module.md) over itself. Every ring has the zero ideal $\{0\}$ and
the ideal $R$ itself; an ideal different from $R$ is called proper.

For a subset $S \subseteq R$, the ideal generated by $S$ is the intersection of
all ideals containing $S$; it consists of the finite sums $a_1 s_1 + \dots + a_n s_n$
with $a_i \in R$ and $s_i \in S$, including the empty sum $0$, and it is the smallest ideal containing $S$:
[it lies inside an ideal $I$ exactly when $S \subseteq I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.span_le)
(see [generating sets](../../../undergraduate/linear-algebra/fundamentals/generating-sets.md)). For instance,
[$z$ lies in the ideal generated by $x$ and $y$ exactly when $z = ax + by$ for some $a, b \in R$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.mem_span_pair).
An ideal generated by a single element $x$ is principal and is written $(x)$ or
$xR$; it consists of the multiples of $x$, since
[$z \in (x)$ exactly when $x$ divides $z$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.mem_span_singleton).

## Examples

- In the [integers](../numbers/integer.md), the multiples of a fixed integer $n$ form the
  ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$. These are all the ideals:
  [every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by a single nonnegative integer](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Norm/AbsNorm.html#Int.ideal_span_absNorm_eq_self).
  The quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$ by $n\mathbb{Z}$, for a natural number $n$, is
  [the ring of integers modulo $n$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Data/ZMod/QuotientRing.html#Int.quotientSpanNatEquivZMod).
- In a [field](../field-theory/field.md) $K$,
  [the only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $K$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Lattice.html#Ideal.eq_bot_or_top), since a nonzero
  element of an ideal can be multiplied by its inverse to give $1$. Conversely,
  [a commutative ring is a field exactly when it has precisely two ideals](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Basic.html#Ring.isField_iff_isSimpleOrder_ideal).
- For a commutative ring $R$ and an element $c \in R$, the
  [polynomials](../polynomials-and-power-series/polynomial-in-one-indeterminate.md) $f \in R[x]$ with $f(c) = 0$ form an ideal,
  the kernel of evaluation at $c$, and
  [this ideal is generated by $x - c$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Ideal.html#Polynomial.ker_evalRingHom).
- In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ the ideal $(2, x)$, made of the polynomials with even
  constant term, is not principal: a generator would have to divide both $2$
  and $x$, so it would be $\pm 1$, but $1 \notin (2, x)$.

## Properties

**Ideals and units.** An ideal is the whole ring as soon as it contains a unit:
[an ideal containing an invertible element is all of $R$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Lattice.html#Ideal.eq_top_of_isUnit_mem),
and in particular [$I = R$ exactly when $1 \in I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Lattice.html#Ideal.eq_top_iff_one).
For principal ideals,
[$(x) = R$ exactly when $x$ is a unit](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.span_singleton_eq_top).

**Divisibility.** Inclusion of principal ideals reverses divisibility:
[$(x) \subseteq (y)$ exactly when $y$ divides $x$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.span_singleton_le_span_singleton).
In an integral domain,
[$(x) = (y)$ exactly when $x$ and $y$ are associates](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Span.html#Ideal.span_singleton_eq_span_singleton),
that is, when $x = uy$ for a unit $u$. Elements $x$ and $y$ are
[coprime](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/coprime-element.md), meaning $ax + by = 1$ for some $a, b \in R$,
[exactly when $(x) + (y) = R$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Operations.html#Ideal.sup_eq_top_iff_isCoprime).

**Operations.** The ideals of $R$ are closed under several operations.

- The intersection $I \cap J$ is an ideal, and so is the intersection of any
  family of ideals: [an element lies in it exactly when it lies in every member](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Lattice.html#Ideal.mem_sInf).
- The sum $I + J$ is the smallest ideal containing both;
  [its elements are the sums $a + b$ with $a \in I$ and $b \in J$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Span/Defs.html#Submodule.mem_sup).
- The product $IJ$ is the ideal generated by the products $ab$ with $a \in I$
  and $b \in J$, so [it contains each such product](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Operations.html#Ideal.mul_mem_mul), and
  [it is contained in $I \cap J$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Operations.html#Ideal.mul_le_inf). For principal ideals,
  [$(x)(y) = (xy)$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Operations.html#Ideal.span_singleton_mul_span_singleton).

**Kernels and quotients.** For a ring morphism $f : R \to S$, the
[kernel](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Maps.html#RingHom.ker), the set of $r \in R$ with
[$f(r) = 0$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Maps.html#RingHom.mem_ker), is an ideal of $R$. More generally the
preimage of an ideal of $S$ is an ideal of $R$, though the image of an ideal
need not be an ideal: the image of $\mathbb{Z}$ in the rational numbers is not
one. Conversely, every ideal $I$ is a kernel: in the quotient ring $R/I$,
[two elements $a$ and $b$ have the same class exactly when $a - b \in I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Defs.html#Ideal.Quotient.eq),
and [the kernel of the quotient map $R \to R/I$ is $I$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Operations.html#Ideal.mk_ker).

**Maximal ideals.** By Zorn's lemma,
[every proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Maximal.html#Ideal.exists_le_maximal),
an ideal that is proper and contained in no larger proper ideal.

## Related concepts

Special kinds of ideals are defined through their quotients: for a commutative
ring, $I$ is a [prime ideal](prime-ideal.md) exactly when
[$R/I$ is an integral domain](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Basic.html#Ideal.Quotient.isDomain_iff_prime), and a
[maximal ideal](maximal-ideal.md) exactly when
[$R/I$ is a field](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Quotient/Basic.html#Ideal.Quotient.maximal_ideal_iff_isField_quotient).
A ring whose ideals are all principal is a principal ideal ring; an integral
domain with this property is a [principal ideal domain](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/principal-ideal-domain.md),
a class that includes every [Euclidean domain](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/euclidean-domain.md). A ring in
which every ideal is generated by finitely many elements is a
[Noetherian ring](../rings/noetherian-ring.md). The
[first isomorphism theorem](first-isomorphism-theorem-for-commutative-rings.md) and the
[Chinese remainder theorem](chinese-remainder-theorem.md) are
statements about ideals and their quotients, and
[fractional ideals](fractional-ideal.md) extend the notion to certain submodules of
the field of fractions of a domain, those that become ideals after
multiplication by a single nonzero element of the domain.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib defines an ideal for any semiring, not only for commutative rings, as a
submodule of the ring viewed as a module over itself. In a noncommutative ring
this is a left ideal; whether it is also closed under multiplication on the right
is a separate property, which holds automatically for every ideal of a
commutative ring, so results stated for two-sided ideals apply to commutative
rings without extra hypotheses. Because ideals are submodules, the general facts
about submodules apply to them directly. The ideals of a ring are ordered by
inclusion and form a complete lattice, in which the zero ideal is the least
element, the whole ring is the greatest, intersection is the meet and sum is the
join; the ideal generated by a set is the span of that set. Images of ideals
under a morphism are defined as the ideal generated by the image, which may be
larger than the image itself.
