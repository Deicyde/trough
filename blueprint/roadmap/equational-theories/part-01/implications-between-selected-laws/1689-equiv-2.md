# 1689 is equivalent to 2

[E1689](https://teorth.github.io/equational_theories/implications/?1689) $({x = (y \diamond x) \diamond ((x \diamond z) \diamond z)})$ is equivalent to [E2](https://teorth.github.io/equational_theories/implications/?2).

## Depends on

- [Equation 1689](../selected-laws/part-02/eq1689.md)
- [Equation 2](../selected-laws/part-01/eq2.md)

## Proof

The implication of [E1689](https://teorth.github.io/equational_theories/implications/?1689) from [E2](https://teorth.github.io/equational_theories/implications/?2) is trivial. The converse is a surprisingly long chain of implications; see pages 326–327 of ([Kisielewicz 1997](https://doi.org/10.1007/s000120050057)). With some computer assistance, we found the following human-readable proof. We denote $y^1=y$ and $y^{n+1}=y^n\diamond y$ for $n\geq 1$. We also introduce the notation

$$
f(x,y) = (x \diamond y) \diamond y , \qquad
g(x,y) = x\diamond f(x,y) = x \diamond ((x \diamond y) \diamond y) .
$$

The initial equation states $x = (y \diamond x) \diamond f(x,z)$. Our main step will be to prove that for all $t\in M$ there exists $w\in M$ such that $f(t,w) = t$. The rest of the proof is then straightforward. Indeed, the initial equation gives $t = (y \diamond t) \diamond f(t,w) = (y \diamond t) \diamond t = f(y,t)$ for any $t,y\in M$. With such a simple expression of $f$ the initial equation becomes $x = (y \diamond x) \diamond z$, which easily implies the singleton law, for instance by writing $x = ((y \diamond w) \diamond x) \diamond z = w \diamond z$ for any $w,x,y,z \in M$.

There remains to prove $f(t,w) = t$ for a well-chosen $w \in M$, explicitly, $w=g(t,t^5)=t\diamond t^7$. For any $t,u,v \in M$, the combinations $x = f(t,u)$ and $y = v \diamond t$ satisfy $y \diamond x = t$. Inserting these values into the initial equation yields the identity

$$
f(t,u) = t \diamond f(f(t,u),z) .\qquad\text{(7)}
$$

Specialize to $z=f(u,v)$ and note that $f(t,u) \diamond z = (\cdots \diamond u) \diamond f(u,v) = u$ by the initial equation so that $f(f(t,u),z) = (f(t,u) \diamond z) \diamond z = u \diamond z = g(u,v)$. Inserting this into (7) yields

$$
f(t,u) = t \diamond g(u,v) .\qquad\text{(8)}
$$

On the one hand, (7) with $z=u=t$ states that $t^3 = t \diamond t^5$, so (using $f(t^n,t)=t^{n+2}$)

$$
f(t,t^5) = (t \diamond t^5) \diamond t^5 = t^3 \diamond t^5 = t^3 \diamond f(t^3,t) = g(t^3,t) ,
$$

and (8) with $(u,v)=(t^3,t)$ then implies $f(t,t^3) = t \diamond g(t^3,t) = t \diamond f(t,t^5) = g(t,t^5)$. On the other hand, (8) with $(u,v)=(t,t^5)$ implies $t^3 = t \diamond g(t,t^5)$. We deduce

$$
f(t,g(t,t^5)) = (t \diamond g(t,t^5)) \diamond g(t,t^5) = t^3 \diamond f(t, t^3) = (\cdots \diamond t) \diamond f(t,\dots) = t .
$$
