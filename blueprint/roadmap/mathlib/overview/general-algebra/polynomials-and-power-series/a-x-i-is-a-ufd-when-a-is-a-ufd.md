# $A[{X_i}]$ is a UFD when $A$ is a UFD

**Polynomial rings over a unique factorisation domain are unique factorisation domains**: if $A$ is a
[unique factorisation domain](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/unique-factorisation-domain.md) (UFD), then so is the
[polynomial ring](polynomial-in-several-indeterminates.md) $A[X_i : i \in I]$ in any family of indeterminates, finite or
infinite. Together with the fact that every [field](../field-theory/field.md) and the ring of
[integers](../numbers/integer.md) are UFDs, it shows that the rings $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and
$\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ have unique factorisation, although most of them are not
[principal ideal domains](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/principal-ideal-domain.md).

## Statement

Let $A$ be a UFD and $I$ any set. Then
[the polynomial ring $A[X_i : i \in I]$ is a UFD](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/UniqueFactorization.html#MvPolynomial.uniqueFactorizationMonoid):
it is an integral domain, every nonzero polynomial that is not a unit is a product of
[irreducible](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/irreducible-element.md) polynomials, and two such factorisations of the same polynomial
have the same factors up to order and multiplication by units. The case of one variable reads:
[if $A$ is a UFD, then so is $A[X]$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/UniqueFactorization.html#Polynomial.uniqueFactorizationMonoid).

The converse also holds: if $A[X_i : i \in I]$ is a UFD, then so is $A$ (trivially so when $I$ is
empty). Degrees add under multiplication over a domain, so a
factorisation of a constant polynomial involves only constants, and unique factorisation in
$A[X_i]$ restricts to unique factorisation in $A$.

## Examples

- **Integer polynomials.** $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD but not a principal ideal domain: the
  [ideal](../ideals-and-quotients/ideal-of-a-commutative-ring.md) generated by $2$ and $X$ is not principal.
- **Several variables over a field.** For a field $k$ and $n \ge 2$, the ring $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$
  is a UFD but not a principal ideal domain: the ideal generated by $X_1$ and $X_2$ is not
  principal. In $\mathbb{Q}[X, Y]$, for instance, $X^2Y - Y^3 = Y(X - Y)(X + Y)$ is the
  factorisation into irreducibles, unique up to the order of the factors and nonzero rational
  constants.
- **The variables themselves.** Over any integral domain,
  [each variable $X_i$ is a prime element](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/MvPolynomial/Division.html#MvPolynomial.prime_X) of the polynomial ring, that
  is, a nonzero nonunit $p$ such that whenever $p$ divides a product $ab$, it divides $a$ or $b$.
- **Infinitely many variables.** $k[X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots]$ is a UFD that is not
  [Noetherian](../rings/noetherian-ring.md): the ideals $(X_1) \subset (X_1, X_2) \subset \cdots$ form
  a strictly increasing chain. The [Hilbert basis theorem](hilbert-basis-theorem.md) gives
  Noetherianity only for
  [finitely many variables over a Noetherian ring](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Basic.html#MvPolynomial.isNoetherianRing); this example
  shows that unique factorisation does not need it.
- **A failure.** $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD, since
  $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, where $2$, $3$ and $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are
  irreducible (the norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$ takes the values $4$, $9$ and $6$ on them
  and never the values $2$ or $3$) and $2$ is not associate to $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$; by the converse above neither is
  $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}][X]$.

## Proof

**One variable.** Let $K$ be the [field of fractions](../field-theory/field-of-fractions-of-an-integral-domain.md) of $A$. Call a
polynomial in $A[X]$ primitive when its coefficients have no common divisor other than units. Every
nonzero $f$ can be written as $f = c f_0$ with $c \in A$ (a [greatest common divisor](../divisibility-in-integral-domains/greatest-common-divisor.md)
of the coefficients) and $f_0$ primitive. Gauss's lemma has three parts, all valid over a UFD:
[the product of two primitive polynomials is primitive](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Content.html#Polynomial.IsPrimitive.mul) (Mathlib
assumes a normalised gcd function on $A$, which every UFD can be given);
[a primitive polynomial is irreducible in $A[X]$ if and only if it is irreducible in $K[X]$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/GaussLemma.html#Polynomial.IsPrimitive.irreducible_iff_irreducible_map_fraction_map);
and [a primitive polynomial that divides another in $K[X]$ already divides it in $A[X]$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/GaussLemma.html#Polynomial.IsPrimitive.dvd_of_fraction_map_dvd_fraction_map).

For existence, factor $c$ in $A$, and factor $f_0$ in $K[X]$, a UFD because
[$K[X]$ is a Euclidean domain](k-x-is-euclidean.md); clearing denominators
turns each factor into a primitive polynomial of $A[X]$, irreducible by the second part of the
lemma, and their product is primitive by the first part. It equals $f_0$ up to a nonzero factor in $K$;
since both are primitive, comparing contents shows that this factor is a unit of $A$. For uniqueness it suffices to show that every irreducible
element is prime. An irreducible constant is a prime of $A$ and stays prime in $A[X]$. An
irreducible nonconstant polynomial is primitive and irreducible in $K[X]$, hence prime there, and
the third part of the lemma brings divisibility back from $K[X]$ to $A[X]$. Finally,
[a domain is a UFD exactly when every nonzero element is a unit times a product of primes](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/UniqueFactorizationDomain/Basic.html#UniqueFactorizationMonoid.iff_exists_prime_factors).

**Finitely many variables.** By induction on $n$, using that
[the ring in $n + 1$ variables is isomorphic to the polynomials in one variable over the ring in $n$ variables](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/MvPolynomial/Equiv.html#MvPolynomial.finSuccEquiv),
and that [a ring isomorphic to a UFD is a UFD](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/UniqueFactorizationDomain/Basic.html#MulEquiv.uniqueFactorizationMonoid).

**Arbitrary variables.** [Every polynomial is the image of a polynomial in finitely many of the variables](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/MvPolynomial/Rename.html#MvPolynomial.exists_finset_rename),
and [for a subset $S$ of $I$, a polynomial in the variables indexed by $S$ is prime in $A[X_i : i \in S]$ if and only if it is prime in $A[X_i : i \in I]$](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/RingTheory/Polynomial/Basic.html#MvPolynomial.prime_rename_iff).
So a factorisation into primes in a finite polynomial ring stays a factorisation into primes in the
large one, and the criterion above applies.

## In Mathlib

Mathlib registers the result as an instance, so the polynomial ring over a UFD is recognised as one
automatically. The indeterminates are indexed by an arbitrary type, with no finiteness assumption,
and the proof for many variables follows the reduction above: finitely many variables by induction,
then the general case because each polynomial involves only finitely many of the variables. Mathlib's notion of unique
factorisation requires cancellation of nonzero factors but not $0 \neq 1$, so the zero ring
qualifies and the theorem holds for it trivially. For one variable Mathlib takes a different route
from the textbook proof: it shows that strict divisibility in $A[X]$ is well-founded (there is no infinite chain of proper
divisors), comparing degrees
and then leading coefficients, and that $A[X]$ has greatest common divisors; in a domain with
greatest common divisors every irreducible element is prime, and these two facts together give
unique factorisation.
